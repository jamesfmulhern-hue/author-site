A Literary Essay · On the Governing Method

The Sacred in the Shabby

How the ordinary — a housedress, a bottle of suntan oil, a classroom of restless teenagers — can take on sacred associations in James Mulhern’s work.

“The sacred is stated in the register of the shabby, and the narrator never once points at it.”

One recurring approach in James Mulhern’s work is more specific than the claim that the sacred is merely hidden in ordinary things. A concordance of 1,096 catalogued passages traces associations across the fiction, poems, and online work: a plain domestic action remains plain, yet may press against a second vocabulary drawn from scripture, liturgy, folk Catholicism, art, and song. The result is not a code in which every object has one fixed answer, but an ordinary world whose objects invite more than one form of notice.

“Assumptions” is the clearest instance. Its epigraph, “You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you,” is not simply a love-poem quotation from the Song of Solomon. In the story’s liturgical setting it is Tota Pulchra Es, the Marian antiphon proper to the Feast of the Assumption, the very day on which the action takes place. The word macula, stain, makes the first close description of Peggy’s sleeves — marked with spilled juice — more than incidental detail. The story places a feast-day promise of unstained beauty against a body already exposed to mockery, illness, dependence, and death.

That pressure does not turn the story into a disguised Mass, Rosary, Stations, or novena. Recent formal work has tested each of those analogies and found that none governs the narrative as a whole. The story’s memories leap across years; the dove, rising, doctrine, and crown arrive in non-devotional order; the adult’s learned explanations interrupt rather than arrange the child’s experience. What endures is a local, cumulative pattern: washing, anointing, carrying, covering, feeding, helping, and remembering are allowed to suggest sacred forms without being stripped of their practical, awkward, or comic reality.

This is why the grandmother’s care matters more than a claim that she is covertly administering a sacrament. Helen can be loving and cruel in the same exchange; Peggy receives assistance and also protects the children; a failed physical cure still yields laughter, touch, and a hand extended in the water. The work’s recurring question is not whether a domestic action can be certified as holy. It is whether dignity can survive when no one involved is fully worthy, competent, or kind.

The pattern travels. In Useless Things and Other Stories, bathing, lifting, rubbing ointment into a heel, feeding, making a safe path, phoning, and asking another person to speak form the book’s actual care catalogue. The collection makes no lasting cure out of them. Attention creates value; it does not reverse decline. In The Weight of Small Mercies, women perform much of the practical ministry, yet ordained men still bless, heal, read scripture, and lead acclamation. The distinction is not between a female Church and an absent male one, but between ceremonial authority and the continuing work of carrying a family.

The newer reading of Give Them Unquiet Dreams adds another restraint. Its dead are not purgatorial souls waiting to be released, and its danger is not a Christian demon. Second sight is hereditary, the dead return under rules of kinship and unfinished relation, and the hostile force is the Irish folk pooka. Catholic consolation and Irish folk belief coexist without being collapsed into one system.

Mulhern’s own classroom essay gives the method its plainest statement. In “Teaching at Fifty-Six,” he names “a sacred self” and calls teaching “communion.” The phrase does not make the classroom an altar. It makes attention to another person’s interior life a serious, daily practice. The fiction and poems make the same claim in more oblique forms: the sacred does not wait for good conditions, and it does not remove the shabby conditions in which it appears.

One paratext states this inheritance more directly than the stories do. Crossings, published by Silver Current Press, carries a dedication to a woman remembered by four things: “your crooked toes from shoes / passed house to house, / your rosary and Daily Missal / that sit on my desk, / your accord with all that life brought, / your devotion to God.” The sequence places poverty, devotional objects, temperament, and belief together — and locates the rosary and missal not in a church but on a writer’s desk. The lines offer a compact image of devotion inherited as an object, kept where work happens, and honored through attention rather than assent.
