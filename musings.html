13 August 2026 Writing I Like

Whitman and Dickinson: two foreparents, two temperaments

I keep these two on the same shelf, though they never met and would likely have unsettled each other if they had. Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson are commonly named the first great American poets, and I think the label holds — not because they wrote alike, but because each, independently and almost simultaneously, broke with English precedent and invented a native way of hearing. Whitman did it by knocking down the wall between the poet and the world. Here he is at the opening of Song of Myself, already refusing the modesty English verse expected of him:

I celebrate myself, and sing myself,
And what I assume you shall assume,
For every atom belonging to me as good belongs to you.

I loafe and invite my soul,
I lean and loafe at my ease observing a spear of summer grass. — Walt Whitman, Song of Myself, 1

Dickinson broke the same wall from the opposite direction — not by enlarging the self until it held everyone, but by narrowing the aperture until a single perception could not be paraphrased away. Where Whitman's line runs long, cumulative, and breathing, hers stops, doubts itself, and starts again on a dash:

I'm Nobody! Who are you?
Are you — Nobody — too?
Then there's a pair of us!
Don't tell! they'd advertise — you know!

How dreary — to be — Somebody!
How public — like a Frog —
To tell one's name — the livelong June —
To an admiring Bog! — Emily Dickinson, 288

Set side by side, the two techniques could hardly be more opposed. Whitman writes in long, unrhymed, incantatory lines built on anaphora and cataloguing — the famous lists of occupations, landscapes, and bodies that pour down the page until the poem feels less like a description of America than an act of ingesting it. His grammar is additive: and, and, and, one clause opening onto the next, because his premise requires that nothing be left out. Dickinson writes in the clipped meter of the hymnal — common meter and ballad meter, the very forms she heard every Sunday in an Amherst pew — but she interrupts that inherited music with slant rhyme, eccentric capitalization, and the dash that has generated a century of scholarly argument. Her grammar is subtractive: she tells the truth, as she says elsewhere, by telling it slant, withholding as much as she reveals. Whitman annexes the world line by line. Dickinson interrogates a single instant until it yields more than it wants to.

Both are among my favorite poets, and I hold them there for almost opposite reasons. I love Dickinson for her intellectuality and her enigmatic lines — the sense that every poem is a locked room with the key visible but just out of reach, and that rereading her is less a pleasure than an ongoing negotiation. I love Whitman for the opposite reason: his expansive self-presentation, his celebratory tone, the way he seems constitutionally incapable of closing a door once he has opened it. I find something quietly ironic in loving them equally, since to love both is to love a contradiction. And yet the contradiction resolves itself, I think, at a deeper level, because both poets are, at bottom, iconoclasts of versification — perseverant and entirely certain of their own craft, unwilling to write the poems their century expected of them. They simply arrived at that certainty from opposite directions. Whitman abolished the line to make room for everyone. Dickinson fractured the line to make room for the one true thing she meant. Both are, underneath the technique, evoking the same American spirit — independence, self-reliance, the conviction that the poem answers to no authority but the poet's own perception.

In today's psychological terms, I would call Dickinson an introvert and Whitman an extrovert, and I do not think the anachronism does either poet a disservice. Whitman's is a poetics of merger — he wants to absorb the crowd and be absorbed by it, to feel the "kelson of the creation" running through every body he catalogues. Dickinson's is a poetics of withdrawal — her speaker is most herself alone, at the window, cataloguing not the world but the mind's report of it. And this is where the two of them stop being merely a matched pair of nineteenth-century originals and start speaking, I think, for two permanent strands of American ideology: the pull toward community and the pull toward being left alone. Whitman is the poet of the "barbaric yawp," the crowded ferry, the "I" that dilates until it contains multitudes. Dickinson is the poet of the shut door, the letter never sent, the self that insists on its right to remain illegible. America has always wanted both things at once — the town meeting and the cabin in the woods — and it is fitting that our two foreparents of poetry embody the choice so completely that neither one alone would do.

I have a more private reason for returning to Dickinson, one that has nothing to do with prosody. I lived for five years a few blocks from her homestead in Amherst, Massachusetts, now a museum, and I passed it often enough that the house stopped being a historical marker and became, in my imagination, simply inhabited. I would look up at her bedroom window and imagine her at it, looking back down at me. Sometimes I glanced into her garden and wished, in the frankly impossible way one wishes for such things, that I could travel back and know her — sit with her, even briefly, in that garden. But I suspect, as the biographical criticism consistently suggests, that any approach of mine would have sent her hurrying back inside, her black dress billowing behind her like dark contrails, the wake of a speeding jet. That, too, seems fitting. The poet who wrote "I'm Nobody" and meant it would hardly have made an exception for an admirer two centuries early or two centuries late.

I did not always make that walk alone. Often my companion past the homestead was Kathy Scherpa, one of the kindest people I have ever known, and I find now that I cherish those unhurried walks even more than the poetry itself — which is a high bar, since I love the poetry a great deal. We would pass the same fence, the same hedge, the same second-floor window, and talk of nothing urgent, which is to say we talked of everything that mattered. I did not know then to call those afternoons some of the best moments of my life. I know it now, plainly, and I suspect Dickinson — who prized nothing so highly as an afternoon rescued from the ordinary and made luminous by attention — would have approved of the accounting, even if she would not have approved of the company.

Whitman, I suspect, would have done the opposite: seen me on the path, called out, and insisted I stay for supper. That, in the end, is the whole difference between them, and the whole reason American poetry needed both.

10 August 2026 From My Notebook

The same doorway, more than once

I have written the same doorway more times than I can count. In “Copacetic” it is the doorway of my father’s hospital room, and he is at the window in the blue bathrobe my sister gave him. In Give Them Unquiet Dreams it is a doorway on Winthrop Street in Braintree, and my grandmother is standing in it with the rosary in one hand and a lit cigarette in the other. In the Aiden stories I am still writing, it is a doorway I have not yet described but that will arrive when it wants to.

For years I thought I was repeating myself — and worried about it, in the way writers are told to worry about it. I have begun, at sixty-three, to think of this less as repetition than as return. There may be a difference. You repeat what you have exhausted. You return to what you have not yet learned to see.

The grandmother in the doorway is the same grandmother, and she has always been the same grandmother. I am not putting her there each time. She is there, and I am learning to look.

I take it as a small consolation that this is not a new theory. Emerson might have called the doorway a “porch.” Chekhov might simply have gone on writing.

28 July 2026 A Borrowed Line

From Marilynne Robinson

I read this passage from Gilead once a year, sometimes twice. It is the letter of an old minister to his young son, and I know few sentences in modern American fiction that carry as much weight so lightly:

“I’ll pray, and then I’ll sleep.” — Marilynne Robinson, Gilead

Five words. The last sentence of the book. What it does is refuse the summing-up that every other closing sentence in the American canon has been reaching for since Fitzgerald’s green light. Robinson trusts the reader to have understood everything. She simply names the two things her narrator is still willing to do: pray, and sleep. In that order.

I sometimes tell students that if they can end a story with two verbs and no explanation, they are already doing something most published writers never manage. Robinson can. Chekhov could. You get there by learning to trust the reader more than you trust your own summary.

14 June 2026 From My Notebook

What I mean by mercy

People ask me, when I say I write from mercy rather than judgment, what mercy has to do with fiction. Fair question. Mercy sounds like something you extend to the deserving. In a story you have made up yourself, no one is deserving because no one is real. You could summarize a character in a sentence and be done with them. Judgment is the easier thing.

But this is what I mean. When I sit down with a character who has done something I do not admire — the father who is drinking, the mother who is failing her children, the boy who has stolen and then lied about it — I try not to have already decided what they are. I try to sit at the kitchen table with them and let them explain themselves in the way they would explain themselves to a friend at four in the morning. Not to me. To a friend. My job, if I do it well, is to have overheard what they said and set it down as accurately as I can.

Mercy in fiction is not forgiveness. It is the willingness to let a character say what they would actually say, in the words they would actually use, without the writer standing behind them with a red pencil marking them as guilty.

This is not, for the record, moral relativism. It is the opposite. It is the belief that every person, in real life, is too complicated to be reduced — and that the writer’s job is to honor that in the invented person too. If the story is good, the reader will do the judging themselves, and their judgment will be more accurate than mine could have been. I get out of the way.

I learned this, or began to learn it, from Chekhov. I try to teach it every semester. I fail at it, in my own work, all the time.

This fragment grew into the essay All We Ever Needed: The Architecture of Mercy, a full study of the short story collection built on this idea.

11 May 2026 From My Notebook

Teaching as the pond I bathe in

A passage I keep returning to — from an essay of mine called "Teaching at Fifty-Six." Written near the end of three decades of teaching inner-city kids, and never far from my desk.

It occurs to me that teaching is my salvation. There are wonderful moments, when things move so fluidly, and the pitch of our discussion seems just right — students are smiling, engaged, sharing feelings, excited. A palpable energy moves through all of us — a realness, an in-the-momentness that charges the air and electrifies our conversations. Connections are made. Questions are answered. We learn who we are. If we are fortunate, we intuit an answer to the question Why? Sometimes we discover what is really true. — From "Teaching at Fifty-Six"

People like to imagine that rare and delectable places lurk in some remote part of the universe or some distant epoch from the past, but right now, in my classroom, surrounded by students, I dwell in that delectable place, a most memorable season of any day. I feel intensely awake, most alive — renewed. This is communion, I think, and teaching will be the pond that I bathe in each morning, every new day.

7 May 2026 A Borrowed Line

From William Trevor

I keep coming back to this passage from Trevor's "The News from Ireland." It is the kind of sentence that teaches you, every time you read it, what the short story is for:

"There is, in his clerical opinion, no part of the world more forsaken by God, no part where charity is more thinly given, kindness more rarely shown." — William Trevor, "The News from Ireland"

What strikes me is the construction. Trevor begins with a tucked-in clause — in his clerical opinion — that identifies the speaker without naming him, and then keeps the sentence moving forward in two parallel halves whose rhythm mimics the very judgment they describe. The sentence becomes the clergyman's mind on the page.

We are taught to admire writers for what they say. We should also admire them for how they hold the reader in a single breath.

18 April 2026 Writing I Like

A stanza by Emily Dickinson

I taught Dickinson for years. I am still teaching her, even now, by reading her in the morning before I write. This is the stanza I came back to this week:

Tell all the truth but tell it slant —
Success in Circuit lies
Too bright for our infirm Delight
The Truth's superb surprise — Emily Dickinson

The instruction is to a poet, but I have come to think it is also an instruction to a novelist. The slant truth is the one the reader can bear; the head-on truth is the one the reader resists. Fiction's whole technology — point of view, scene, dialogue, metaphor, the careful arrangement of what is shown and what is withheld — is in service of telling it slant.

Dickinson knew at thirty what most of us are still working out at sixty.
