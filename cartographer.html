A Critical Essay · Original to This Site

The Quiet Cartographer of Irish-American Memory

How James Mulhern’s fiction and poetry chart the same small set of coordinates — a kitchen, a threshold, a shoreline — across genres and decades.

James Mulhern’s fiction and poetry keep returning to the same small set of coordinates: a grandmother’s kitchen outside Boston, a church’s stained glass, a beach across from an amusement park, a dock where water cannot quite be reached. Read across his story collections, his novels, and the poems of Crossings, these places accumulate into something like a map — not of geography exactly, but of an inherited Irish-American Catholic memory, charted patiently, poem by poem and story by story, rather than declared all at once.

The map’s coastline is Boston and its working-class suburbs — Revere, Wollaston, Nantasket — rendered without romance. Molly Bonamici’s narrator calls her hometown “a dismal working-class city, with stickball courts, hockey games, small backyards with vegetable gardens, and statues of the Madonna on front lawns,” a landscape whose modesty is itself part of the inheritance being charted. The poems of Crossings extend this cartography backward across the Atlantic, to a threshold in “Dark City” where a grandfather’s emigration is rendered as literal passage: “I see you move from darkness into the light and beyond.”

What makes this body of work cartographic rather than merely regional is its insistence on returning to the same landmarks from new angles across different books. A grandmother’s kitchen recurs in “Assumptions” and in poems like “Catherine” and “The Teapot.” A church’s threshold recurs in “The Guard” and in the sacramental vocabulary that runs through the poetry more broadly. A dog rehomed, a father’s last hours, a piano destroyed in anger — each incident marked once becomes a coordinate the later work quietly revisits, the way a cartographer revises the same territory as new surveys come in.

This is memory work conducted at close range rather than from an aerial view: Mulhern’s method surveys a family’s interior rooms and a neighborhood’s street corners rather than a nation’s broad Irish-American narrative. The result is a body of work that functions, across genres, as a kind of quiet map — not a monument to heritage, but a patient, close-grained record of where an inherited faith, an inherited grief, and an inherited tenderness actually lived.
