A Critical Essay · Original to This Site

The Quiet Cartographer of Irish-American Memory

How a fixed set of kitchens, shorelines, and thresholds — tracked poem to story to novel — forms one continuous, deliberately declared map across James Mulhern’s work.

James Mulhern’s fiction and poetry keep returning to the same small set of coordinates: a grandmother’s kitchen outside Boston, a boulder battered by foam at Clew Bay, a beach across from an amusement park, a pair of shoes handed down until they leave a child’s toes crooked. Read across his story collections, his novels, and the poems of Crossings, these places and objects are not incidental returns but a single, declared system — the author’s own image for it is a good chair “moved from novel to poem to story the way any person moves the good chair from one room to another when the light changes.” The furniture does not multiply. It is only ever rearranged, and the rearranging is the map.

The map’s coastline is a specific, unglamorous Boston: Jamaica Plain, Mission Hill, Braintree, Dorchester, and, in the Italian-American variation of Molly Bonamici, Revere. Grandmothers clean houses “for the filthy rich on the hill”; grandfathers dig graves by day and haul mailbags at South Station by night. Beneath the geography runs a specific class wound, a storefront placard reading “No Irish Need Apply” that recurs verbatim across the poems and the fiction — not nostalgia, but an inquiry into how a persecuted people built a code of family first, and what that code costs. Molly Bonamici’s narrator calls her own hometown “a dismal working-class city, with stickball courts, hockey games, small backyards with vegetable gardens, and statues of the Madonna on front lawns” — a landscape whose modesty is itself part of the inheritance being surveyed.

Within that coastline, the kitchen table is where every real reckoning happens. Lies are told there, prayers said there, confessions made there — a habit visible from the Aiden stories to the marinara-and-whiskey kitchen councils of Molly Bonamici. Beside the table sits the map’s smallest and most freighted object: a pair of shoes. In “Shoes,” “She Knelt,” and across the poems, the immigrant mother cleans houses for the rich and brings home discarded shoes that are too small, leaving the next generation with crooked toes — class, inheritance, and love-through-sacrifice compressed into a single, wearable object. Nearby, an aging, failing-sighted mother “traces the shape of my face” with her fingers, a gesture that recurs until it becomes the work’s own definition of love: knowing by touch what the eyes can no longer see.

The map’s truest destination, though, is water. The sea is where Mulhern’s writing goes at the end — the Assumption “cure” at Nantasket, ashes scattered at Keem Bay, Molly’s final swim toward home. One image in particular, tracked across four separate texts, teaches the method more efficiently than any definition could: the grandmother, as a girl, “swimming toward a boulder battered by foam” to gather sea lettuce at Clew Bay. The image recurs nearly verbatim in the poem “Scrape the Rocks,” in the story “A Bath,” and again in the novels — the same girl, the same boulder, the same foam, surfacing in four different genres across perhaps twenty years of writing. This is cartography in the strictest sense: not a metaphor invoked once, but a coordinate the author keeps returning to, checking, and re-drawing.

The map extends across the Atlantic as well. In the poem “Dark City,” a two-year-old’s single memory of a great-grandfather standing in a “dark hallway” in Boston is carried forward to an adult speaker standing at that same man’s abandoned homestead in Ireland, pressing “my palm against the homestead wall, / as you touched my head so many years ago.” The touched head becomes the touching hand: recurrence rendered as a physical rhyme rather than a verbal one, the emigrant’s journey folded into a single gesture repeated a generation and an ocean apart.

The poems of Crossings are the purest distillation of this cartography — everything the novels do at length, the poems do in twenty lines, isolating one object, one memory, one threshold before the fiction sets it in motion. “Copacetic” gives a father in a doorway asking if everything is “copacetic,” a spoonful of untouched Jell-O, and a robe left “folded, on the table by the door” — images that later become the emotional architecture of the story “The Wrong Way” and of the father’s death in Give Them Unquiet Dreams. “Shoes” and “She Knelt” supply the immigrant backstory — the burlap bag of discarded shoes, the “No Irish Need Apply” storefront, the crooked toes — that stands behind every book. “Moving Forward” gives the blind mother whose “fingers trace my face,” and “Leaves,” in which a dead father whispers “We’re done” at his own graveside, is the visitation motif in embryo. As the chapbook’s own introduction puts it: “My family members are my poems. My poems are my prayers.” The prose dramatizes what the poems distill; the poems pray what the prose narrates.

Other landmarks recur with the same deliberateness. At the close of both “Blindfolded” and Give Them Unquiet Dreams, a dead grandfather sits beneath a tree and “one star shone in the twilit sky” — hope made visible, and named as such when a mother identifies the figure beneath the tree as “Hope” and the grandmother answers, “Hope is sitting right beside me.” A mother in the Aiden stories copies George Frederic Watts’s 1886 painting Hope, a blindfolded woman on a globe playing a lyre with a single remaining string — second sight compressed into one emblem. A stolen sacred object — a chalice, a rosary, a set of keys, a string of pearls — is returned a story later, the theft-and-return itself a small demonstration that “a theft becomes a return.” A pocket watch, drawn and re-drawn, is inscribed on its reverse with scripture keyed to the hour it shows: 3:01 paired with Ecclesiastes 3:1, 12:07 with Ecclesiastes 12:7, the abstraction of eternity made into a touchable object. And snow, borrowed openly from Joyce’s “The Dead,” falls at moments of reckoning throughout — a father “going the wrong way” in a storm, a classroom discussion of Joyce’s story with snow tapping the glass.

Nearly every piece in this body of work ends the same way: at a threshold, toward water. A deathbed, a grave, a departure, a swim into open water — the true subject was never death itself but what love does with it. Taken together, these are not separate motifs so much as one motif photographed from different angles: a kitchen, a pair of shoes, a boulder at Clew Bay, a stopped watch, a star over a tree, all instruments of a single conviction that nothing loved is ever finished happening.

Mulhern has, in his own framing, built a single imaginative house and furnished it with a finite set of beloved objects, then written, patiently, from room to room — a story is one room, a novel a larger one, a poem a window. The grandmother of the Aiden stories walks, under an Italian name, through Molly Bonamici; the Nantasket beach that cures one family cures another; the drowned brother of a novel is the “stolen child” of a poem on the last page. The recurrence, on this reading, is declared rather than disguised — an invitation to feel what the author believes: that the dead are in the next room, that past, present, and future are one block of time through which our attention moves like a flashlight, and that a good chair, carried carefully enough from room to room, means more each time it is set down. A fuller treatment of this method — including a complete map of its recurring images across every book — appears in the critical study The Writing Style of James Mulhern (Silver Current Press).
