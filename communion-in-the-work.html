A Literary Essay · On Recurring Imagery

Communion in the Work

Communion in teaching, family care, the pelican emblem, and poems for the dead — with ordained ministry and Irish folk belief kept in view.

“This is communion, and teaching will be the pond that I bathe in each morning, every new day.” — “Teaching at Fifty-Six,” The Font: A Literary Journal for Language Teachers

Communion, in James Mulhern’s work, is named most directly in “Teaching at Fifty-Six”: “This is communion, and teaching will be the pond that I bathe in each morning, every new day.” The word appears in a classroom rather than at an altar, but the claim is not that the classroom simply replaces the sacrament. It is that sustained attention to another person’s mind can carry a communal and spiritual seriousness that institutional language alone does not exhaust.

The pelican scene in the novel Give Them Unquiet Dreams gives the religious vocabulary its sharpest emblem. A character tells a child that “A pelican is a symbol of Jesus… a mother pelican will wound herself, striking her breast with her beak to feed the babies.” The medieval pelican-in-her-piety is an established Eucharistic image. Mulhern places that image in a child’s hearing as an ordinary piece of family knowledge, where it becomes one instance in a larger practice of passing religious language through domestic talk.

“Assumptions” works at a different scale. On the Feast of the Assumption, under the Marian antiphon Tota Pulchra Es, a family feeds, carries, anoints, covers, and helps Peggy into and out of the water. These actions can suggest sacramental forms, but the story is not arranged as an Order of Mass or a Rosary sequence. Its actual rhythm is irregular and remembered: help is repeated, care passes between people, and no one secures a cure. The point is not to declare a beach trip a liturgy; it is to see how service survives without ceremony.

The Weight of Small Mercies makes this distinction especially clear. Women and family members perform much of the continuing pastoral work: intercession, touch, washing, anointing, feeding, rescue, practical care, and protective silence. Yet ordained men are present. They bless, heal, read scripture, and lead acclamation. The correction matters because the book does not erase clerical ministry; it shows that ceremonial acts do not carry the family’s daily burden of care. Nor does any system of healing — clerical, medical, folk, or domestic — yield a final guarantee.

The work extends communion across death, but not by making every dead figure a Christian soul in need of release. In Give Them Unquiet Dreams, second sight is hereditary, good dead return to complete relational work, and the hostile force is the Irish folk pooka. Catholic prayer for the dead and folk belief coexist. The novel’s communion across death is therefore relational and local, not a simple purgatorial mechanism.

The poems give this practice its most compact formulation: “My family members are my prayers. My poems are my prayers. My family members are my poems.” A poem for the dead neither proves redemption nor substitutes for a rite. It continues attention where speech and ordinary repair have ended. Across the corpus, communion names that costly practice: listening, carrying, feeding, remembering, and refusing to treat a life as finished when its usefulness, health, or ease has failed.

The lay sacrament is also, it turns out, the part an editor will cut. “Blindfolded” survives complete in one magazine and appears elsewhere as a truncated fragment that opens mid-scene and drops the entire chalice sequence — with it the invented miracle in which a healing is likened to “the water that gushed from the rock at Rephidim and Kadesh,” and the congregation’s answering “Thanks be to God.” Whoever shortened the story treated the sacramental machinery as the detachable part and kept the plot. That judgement is worth recording, because it is exactly inverted: the theft is the plot’s engine, but the borrowed liturgical language is what makes a boy’s errand into an act with consequences beyond itself.

Compression tells the same story from the other side. Length is not what determines whether the devotional apparatus survives — genre is. A comic-horror story of sixteen hundred words, epigraphed from Poe, carries no religious material whatever, and there is no reason it should. A fourteen-line elegy compresses a whole arc from Communion to adulthood into one gesture: a man who says nothing and crosses himself in the light of a dashboard. The short forms do not thin the sacramental habit. They concentrate it wherever the mode is elegiac, and drop it entirely wherever the mode is comic, which suggests the habit belongs to grief rather than to doctrine.
