A Literary Essay · On Recurring Imagery

Communion in the Work

Communion in teaching, family care, the pelican emblem, and poems for the dead — with ordained ministry and Irish folk belief kept in view.

“This is communion, and teaching will be the pond that I bathe in each morning, every new day.” — “Teaching at Fifty-Six,” The Font: A Literary Journal for Language Teachers

Communion, in James Mulhern’s work, is named most directly in “Teaching at Fifty-Six”: “This is communion, and teaching will be the pond that I bathe in each morning, every new day.” The word appears in a classroom rather than at an altar, but the claim is not that the classroom simply replaces the sacrament. It is that sustained attention to another person’s mind can carry a communal and spiritual seriousness that institutional language alone does not exhaust.

The pelican scene in the novel Give Them Unquiet Dreams offers a clear emblem for this religious vocabulary. A character tells a child that “A pelican is a symbol of Jesus… a mother pelican will wound herself, striking her breast with her beak to feed the babies.” The medieval pelican-in-her-piety is an established Eucharistic image. Mulhern places that image in a child’s hearing as an ordinary piece of family knowledge, where it becomes one instance in a larger practice of passing religious language through domestic talk.

“Assumptions” works at a different scale. On the Feast of the Assumption, under the Marian antiphon Tota Pulchra Es, a family feeds, carries, anoints, covers, and helps Peggy into and out of the water. These actions can suggest sacramental forms, but the story is not arranged as an Order of Mass or a Rosary sequence. Its actual rhythm is irregular and remembered: help is repeated, care passes between people, and no one secures a cure. The point is not to declare a beach trip a liturgy; it is to see how service survives without ceremony.

The Weight of Small Mercies makes this distinction especially clear. Women and family members perform much of the continuing pastoral work: intercession, touch, washing, anointing, feeding, rescue, practical care, and protective silence. Yet ordained men are present. They bless, heal, read scripture, and lead acclamation. The correction matters because the book does not erase clerical ministry; it shows that ceremonial acts do not carry the family’s daily burden of care. Nor does any system of healing — clerical, medical, folk, or domestic — yield a final guarantee.

The work extends communion across death, but not by making every dead figure a Christian soul in need of release. In Give Them Unquiet Dreams, second sight is hereditary, good dead return to complete relational work, and the hostile force is the Irish folk pooka. Catholic prayer for the dead and folk belief coexist. The novel’s communion across death is therefore relational and local, not a simple purgatorial mechanism.

The poems express this practice concisely: “My family members are my prayers. My poems are my prayers. My family members are my poems.” A poem for the dead neither proves redemption nor substitutes for a rite. It continues attention where speech and ordinary repair have ended. Across the corpus, communion names a practice of listening, carrying, feeding, remembering, and refusing to treat a life as finished when its usefulness, health, or ease has failed.

One version of “Blindfolded” leaves out material that another version retains. The complete magazine text includes a chalice sequence and an invented miracle in which a healing is likened to “the water that gushed from the rock at Rephidim and Kadesh,” followed by the congregation’s “Thanks be to God.” The shortened version keeps the plot while omitting this liturgical language. That editorial choice is worth noting, because readers may differ on whether the religious material is detachable from the boy’s errand or helps give it a wider resonance.

Compression offers a related observation. Length is not necessarily what determines whether devotional material appears; genre may matter more. A comic-horror story of sixteen hundred words, epigraphed from Poe, carries no religious material, while a fourteen-line elegy moves from Communion to adulthood in one gesture: a man who says nothing and crosses himself in the light of a dashboard. In these short forms, sacramental material appears more readily in the elegiac mode and not at all in the comic one, suggesting that the habit may be tied more closely to grief than to doctrine.

Who gets to speak this language is itself worth noting. In the stories, sacred speech is not evenly shared. It belongs largely to the old, and to women: a grandmother whose nightly intention is offered for the pope’s, an Italian grandmother who keeps the oaths and the Marian dialect while declining the belief. Younger characters use the vocabulary too, but differently — one calls teaching a vocation, another reads the Prologue of John closely while professing no faith at all. A New Age healer runs the same devotional economy as salesmanship. The language circulates freely; what varies is what each speaker is doing with it.

The crisis scenes may be the more telling evidence. When someone actually dies in these stories, the liturgy tends not to arrive. A collapse in a corridor gets CPR and panic. One death gets a secular last word, another a pop song, another the procedure of police at a scene. Whatever consolation is available comes afterward and in plainer form. The last story ends not with a prayer but with an apology answered by a woman saying she is also responsible — which is, in this collection’s terms, the more dependable article. Sacred speech remains alive throughout; it is simply that attention, admission and careful listening are what the stories trust when the moment is real.
