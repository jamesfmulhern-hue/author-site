An Essay · On Religious Interpretation

Altogether Beautiful

Reading “Assumptions” against the Catholic dogma of the Assumption — and asking what it means to grant a flawed, ordinary body the imagery reserved for the sinless one.

“Tota pulchra es, amica mea, et macula non est in te.” — Song of Solomon 4:7 in the Vulgate; epigraph to “Assumptions”

The title carries two meanings at once. “Assumptions” names the small cruelties of a family that has already decided, before the beach trip even starts, what Peg is worth — “the homeliest damn woman” in the family, good for a joke, not much else. But capitalized, in Catholic usage, an Assumption is a specific doctrine: the teaching, defined by Pope Pius XII in the 1950 apostolic constitution Munificentissimus Deus, that the Virgin Mary was taken up body and soul into heavenly glory at the close of her earthly life, spared the ordinary corruption of the grave. The story explores the tension between these senses of its title, asking whether the second sense — a body received into glory rather than left to decay — can be imagined in relation to a body that has already failed every test implied by the first.

The epigraph is the antiphon of the feast

The verse that opens the story is not chosen for its sweetness. In the Vulgate, Song of Solomon 4:7 reads Tota pulchra es, amica mea, et macula non est in te. From the fourth century onward that verse, with Mary’s name substituted for the beloved’s, has been sung as Tota pulchra es, Maria — one of the oldest Marian prayers in the Latin Church. In the older chant books it is assigned to two feasts. One of them is August 15: the Feast of the Assumption, which is the day on which the story takes place.

So the epigraph is not a poetic flourish laid over a summer outing. It is the proper chant of the liturgical day the plot occupies. And it supplies the story’s single most important word. Macula means stain. Immaculate means unstained.

Now read the first close description of Peggy’s body on the morning of that feast:

“Her housedress, which had a pattern of tiny roses, shrouded a pair of small black boots; there were red stains at the end of her sleeves from where she had spilled some juice.” — “Assumptions”

The epigraph promises a body without stain. The body arrives marked — in red, at the ends of the sleeves, which is to say at the wrists, which is where the nails go. The dress that carries the stains is patterned with roses, the flower of the Litany of Loreto and of the Passion. And the dress does not cover the boots; it shrouds them, three pages before the woman wearing it will need a shroud.

None of this is announced. The narrator supplies an innocuous explanation in the same clause — spilled juice — and moves on. One possible reading is that the story places sacred associations in the language of the shabby without having a character point them out.

The sacramental sequence disguised as a day at the beach

Once the pattern is visible, the Nantasket excursion reads as a sequence of sacraments performed by people who would be astonished to hear them called that. Tanning oil is rubbed into a bald scalp, forehead, and neck — and oil poured on the head is what makes a king, a priest, an anointed one; it is also what the woman with the alabaster jar did at Bethany, of which Christ said she had done it beforehand for his burial. Peggy dies within days.

A gust lifts her wig; a child catches it and holds it up “like the head of John the Baptist”; the narrator smooths it and buries it in a paper bag with a stone he has found near the water. A head, a shroud, and a stone: an entombment, performed as play, on the morning of the feast. What is buried is the false hair. What comes out of the sea an hour later is the true head, anointed and shining.

A mourning dove starts from a cleft in the rocks, rises, and then descends over the water at the exact spot where two women are wading in. That is the Baptism of the Jordan, and the cleft in the rock is Song of Songs 2:14. A wave nobody asked for knocks both women flat and leaves them kneeling in the surf, laughing until they cry — a total immersion administered by force, arriving at the posture of prayer by accident. And when a boy reaches down and she takes his hand, the sentence reads: she rose from the sea.

Judgment’s imagery, reissued to mercy

One striking element of the story is not simply its use of religious imagery but its altered emphasis. Nothing abject is removed; images of condemnation can be reread in the light of mercy or glory.

  • Unquenchable fire, the Gospels’ own phrase for hell, becomes sunlight glittering on the water as two women walk into the sea.
  • Baldness, a curse in Isaiah, becomes “a laurel of peach-fuzz hair” garlanded at the temples — a crown, and behind it the Coronation that follows the Assumption.
  • The stigmata become spilled juice on a housedress sleeve.
  • Chrism becomes suntan lotion; the entombment becomes a paper bag; the purple robe of the mocked king becomes a beach towel spread over two children so they will not burn.
  • And the Cross appears in the final sentence as a Father’s “crossed arms” in the act of cradling a dead woman’s head and kissing her cheek — the instrument of death disclosed as the shape of an embrace.

Not one story: a body of work

The pattern is not confined to a single story, though its extent remains open to interpretation. A line-by-line survey of the whole corpus — two novels, three story collections, the poems, and twenty-eight pieces published in literary magazines — has catalogued 1,191 passages associated with scripture, liturgy, hagiography, iconography, and a literary line running from Yeats and Joyce through Dante to Flannery O’Connor.

Four kinds of evidence hold the case together.

The doctrine is quoted, not gestured at. The text of the 1950 constitution appears inside a short story. Mark 15:33–34 and 16:6 are read verbatim over two caskets in Molly Bonamici. John 1:1, Matthew 7:6, Romans 6:23, and Genesis 1:26–28 all appear directly.

The same images recur for three decades. The Feast of the Assumption. A seawater cure at Nantasket. A wet silvery scalp and a crown the boy will not touch. A mourning dove out of its cleft. “Up you go. On the count of three.” Twenty-four such repetitions have been documented across the books, sometimes in identical sentences — a private liturgy, returned to on a cycle the way a missal returns to the same propers every August.

Revision runs toward the sacred, not away from it. A poem was changed from “At dawn, he was gone” to “When I wake, I find that he has gone,” moving the resurrection parallel off objective paschal dawn and onto the mourner’s own waking. Another draft sharpens into the grammar of Psalm 130. A third swaps embers for paper and keeps the smoke rising. A magazine reprint cut the Lughnasadh material and the line about hell; the book keeps them.

The pattern may also be absent. One story in The Weight of Small Mercies lacks the transfiguring apparatus entirely — no ghost, no holy object, no cure, no scriptural frame — and has a notably bleak tone within the cycle. The contrast offers a useful check on this interpretation.

An Irish Catholicism of the small mercy

There is no functioning priest anywhere in these books who does what a priest is for. A grandmother invents a nickname after a sermon on the power of names and thereby confers a grace that reaches, by the last page, into the Trinity. Another leads the blessing. Another absolves on a park bench. Another administers a counterfeit Eucharist to a woman in an institution. The men are dead, absent, silent, drunk, or at work. The Church in this fiction is a matriarchy operating without a license, and it works.

And characters who deny God outright may still grieve in Catholic language. The stories suggest people for whom Catholicism has stopped being true without ceasing to be an available language.

Canonization, not catharsis

Catharsis purges. This work converts. The stain stays and becomes a wound; drunkenness stays and becomes Pentecost; a gold crucifix is dismissed as “a useless thing,” which is Saint Paul’s own doctrine — God chose the foolish things, and the despised things, and the things which are not.

In 1950 a pope closed a document with the words we pronounce, declare, and define. The story closes: Completely awake—laughing, altogether beautiful, and divine—she rises once again to sing her favorite song. Glory, for Peggy Fleming, is a Friday night set in a Back Bay dive with her husband at a table near the front.

The closing language recalls the pope’s formulation, spoken by a boy about a woman others have treated unkindly. Tota pulchra es. There is no flaw in you.

What makes that ending land is partly what the story never says. Set on the Feast of the Assumption, it withholds almost the entire vocabulary of the thing it performs. There is no altar, no amen, no rosary, no mercy, no sacrifice, no resurrection, no veil, no baptism, no canonization. Even grace and spirit never appear as nouns — the story allows only “gracious” and “spirited,” the adjectives, kept down at the level of manners and temperament. In their place stand folk substitutes and plain speech: “Mary’s blessing,” “a cure,” “miracles,” “Lovely Peggy,” and a repeated practical vocabulary of getting wet, helping, and rising. A story that stages a canonization refuses the nouns that would declare one.

The ordinary words carry the weight instead, and they arrive in the compromised sense first. Body is a judged physical body long before it is “body and soul.” Cure begins as a folk rite and slides into joints, bowels, and wet boots. Cross is mostly a verb for getting across a street or a threshold. Wine is never consecrated; it is a smell in a kitchen. White does five different jobs, and the first is a white lie — so that by the time clothing is “white as snow,” the reader is being asked to hold the harmless deception inside the exalted image rather than replace one with the other. That is the story’s actual method: not sacred language applied to a shabby life, but household language put under pressure until it will bear a sacred reading without ever announcing that it has.
