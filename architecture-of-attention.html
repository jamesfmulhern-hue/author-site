A Close Reading · On the Whole Body of Work

The Architecture of Attention

Craft, motif, theme, and allusion across the fiction, poetry, and teaching of James Mulhern — a synthesis of the techniques that make his recurrences a coherent, disciplined vision rather than a set of habits.

Abstract Mulhern has written, of his own method, that he “place[s] the ordinary and the unspeakable in the same paragraph” and trusts that a detail, “watched closely enough and long enough,” will “tell me what it is actually for.”1 This essay reads that stated method against the demonstrable evidence of the corpus, synthesizing findings developed in greater depth across the site's other essays into a single account of four interlocking systems: a sentence-level and structural craft signature (missing dialogue tags, action-beat attribution, second sight as a licensed point-of-view exemption, genetic revision visible across published texts); a set of recurring tropes (fire, water, thresholds, sacred objects hidden inside shabby ones); a six-part thematic architecture (faith and doubt, family as fortress and prison, the sacred ordinary, inherited guilt, the ethics of listening, the persistence of the dead); and a disciplined allusive practice in which names, scripture, and canonical texts are deployed as compressed, load-bearing citations rather than ornament. Taken together, these systems constitute what this essay calls an architecture of attention — a body of work organized less around plot mechanics than around the sustained, disciplined act of looking hard at what most fiction considers beneath its notice.

I. A Stated Method, Tested Against the Text

Critics writing about a living author have an advantage rarely available to critics of the dead: the author has, in this case, described his own method directly, and that description can be checked against the work rather than merely inferred from it. Mulhern's introduction to All We Ever Needed states that “I take language to be a sacred inheritance and the act of naming to be a kind of devotion”;2 his essay on craft states that his habit is “to place the ordinary and the unspeakable in the same paragraph — a teacup poured, a lie confessed; a hand laid on a bald scalp, a body quietly dying under it,” and that he trusts a closely watched detail to “tell me what it is actually for,” adding that “it is very rarely only what it appears to be.”1 He describes revision not as correction but as “listening harder to a draft that already knows more than I did when I wrote it,”1 and his credo names attention itself, rather than any particular belief, as the discipline his work is built to practice: “Our failure, then, is not principally a failure of faith but of attention.”3

These statements of method offer a useful lens for the work. This essay considers whether the missing dialogue tags, recurring fire and water, thematic concerns, and citational names might be read together as related habits operating at the scales of the sentence, scene, book, and broader body of work.

II. Craft Signature: The Sentence-Level Techniques

At the level of the individual sentence, Mulhern's fiction often favors a small set of techniques that a companion essay on this site discusses in detail: limited use of explanatory dialogue tags, physical action beats in place of “he said”/“she said,” and the em dash for self-interruption and withheld speech.4 In stories including “A Bath” and “Myra Bocca,” these choices can make dialogue feel closer to overheard exchange, leaving the placement of a dash or the length of a line to carry information that adverbs and commentary might otherwise provide.4 The approach resembles the author’s stated preference for allowing material to stand without immediate interpretation.

A related withholding appears in Mulhern's handling of first-person confession and justification. A companion essay identifies a recurring narrative posture the site calls the unreliable confession: a first-person narrator — the poisoner of “Peppermint Candy,” the killer in “Mia Bambina” — who reports morally catastrophic action in the same flat, procedural diction used for filing paperwork or pouring orange juice, offering justification without a narratorial signal that the justification should be doubted.5 Rather than declaring their unreliability, these narrators can sound reasonable, leaving readers to register the gap between the calm register and the content being described.

III. Structural Craft: Second Sight, Compression, and the Point-of-View Exemption

In Give Them Unquiet Dreams, the device of second sight provides a sustained way of managing access to information. A companion essay reads it not primarily as theme but as narrative technology: a feature of the novel’s world that permits foreshadowing, brings three generations and two continents of family history into scenes a fourteen-year-old narrator can plausibly witness, and connects a supernatural premise to the plot concerning a mother’s psychiatric commitment.6 The device addresses a familiar challenge of first-person narration — how a temporally bound consciousness can encounter information beyond ordinary realistic access — through material that draws on Irish folk cosmology rather than flashback chapters or omniscient narration.6

Related questions arise in Mulhern's child and adolescent narrators. A companion essay argues that Molly Bonamici and the young Aiden Glencar may be unreliable less because their perception fails than because their affect is withheld: they report what they see while lacking, or declining, the interpretive frame to say what it costs them. Because both novels are narrated from an adult retrospective vantage, this withholding can work on two temporal tracks — what the child did not understand in the moment, and what the adult narrator, looking back, still leaves unexplained.7 This approach lets a coming-of-age narrative remain unsettled between childhood innocence and retrospective understanding.

IV. Recurring Tropes: Fire, Water, and the Sacred Hidden in the Shabby

Across the fiction and poetry, a small set of images recur with enough consistency that the site's essays treat them as a compositional system rather than coincidence: fire, which moves in Mulhern's work from a scriptural register of damnation into “the shimmer over a baptism”;8 water, which carries “tears, rain, tide pools, and the unglamorous water of the sickroom” as the element that transmits mercy across the corpus;9 and thresholds — doorways, crosswalks, the border between sleep and waking — that recur so often across the poetry and fiction that a companion essay reads them as a governing structural device rather than incidental staging.10 Underneath all three tropes runs a single governing conviction the site's essays call the sacred in the shabby: “the holy in James Mulhern's work always arrives disguised as the ordinary — a housedress, a bottle of suntan oil, a classroom of restless teenagers.”11 This is not merely a thematic preference; it is, per the author's own account of his working method, the actual mechanism by which his images are generated — he does not begin with an intended symbol and search for an object to carry it, but returns obsessively to a concrete image (a housedress, a beach, a bottle of oil) until, in his words, “the language underneath the language declared itself.”1

V. The Thematic Architecture: Six Pillars

A full-length comparative study on this site identifies six thematic pillars running through the corpus: faith and doubt; family as both fortress and prison; the ordinary understood as sacred; the persistence of the dead among the living; inherited guilt passed forward across generations; and the ethics of unauthorized confession — who is granted the authority to hear what cannot otherwise be said, and what that authority costs the listener.12 These six pillars are demonstrably not independent thematic preoccupations that happen to recur in the same author's work; they are structurally interdependent, each pillar supplying the conditions the others require. Family functions as prison specifically because it is also fortress — the same intimacy that protects a character from the outside world (Aiden shielding Laura from psychiatric scrutiny, Billy shielding his mother's silence in “A Bath”) is what makes betrayal from within the family so costly. Inherited guilt persists specifically because the dead persist — Sinead's death by the well does not resolve into the past tense because Catherine's guilt, and later Aiden's inherited obligation to close the account with Rita, keep it structurally present across three generations.13 And the ethics of confession matter specifically because none of Mulhern's confessors — grandmothers, children, a boy speaking for his dead grandmother — hold institutional warrant for the power they exercise, which is what makes each confessional scene a live moral question rather than a settled sacrament.14

VI. Allusive Practice: Names, Scripture, and Citation as Compression

Mulhern's allusive practice operates chiefly through naming, and a companion essay on this site demonstrates that his character names function as compressed citations rather than euphonious labels: Molly Bonamici imports Defoe's Moll Flanders, a survivor narrating her own moral compromises in flat, unsentimental first person, into a surname that means “good friends” and is worn by a woman who kills, calmly, out of family loyalty.15 Myra Bocca imports Fitzgerald's Myrtle Wilson — killed by a car in The Great Gatsby — through near-homophony, so that a reader who catches the echo has effectively been told the plot in advance, while “Bocca” (Italian for “mouth”) literalizes the character's compulsive talkativeness before she speaks a line.16 Peggy Fleming imports the 1968 Olympic figure skater's name into a body devastated by disease and alcoholism, a cruelty the text names explicitly, while quietly preserving in the character one faculty — performed grace under difficult conditions — that the allusion's source figure was famous for.17 This is allusion functioning as the author's own stated principle of naming as devotion made technical: each name is a promise the narrative goes on to keep, ironize, or literalize, and the promise is legible only to a reader willing to hear names as citations rather than sounds.

A second allusive register operates through direct citation of religious and canonical material: the Marian dogma of the Assumption structuring “Assumptions”'s religious architecture,18 the Emerson-and-Thoreau classroom scene in Give Them Unquiet Dreams naming Mulhern's Transcendentalist inheritance directly,19 and W. B. Yeats's Celtic Twilight supplying the folkloric taxonomy — banshees, second sight, the porousness of the dead and the living — that Mulhern relocates from rural Sligo into Irish-American Boston.20 In every register, allusion in Mulhern's work does compression's job: it lets a single name or a single borrowed image carry a weight of inherited meaning the text itself does not have to spell out, precisely the same economy his stated method claims for the ordinary object watched closely enough to reveal what it is actually for.

VII. Genetic Evidence: Revision as a Visible Process

Because Mulhern's fiction and poetry have been published, republished, and adapted across multiple texts over more than a decade, the corpus offers something most contemporary fiction does not: visible genetic evidence of the author's own revision process. A companion essay on this site documents a bath scene redrafted from poem to story and a jetty passage duplicated within a single collection, arguing that these are not oversights but evidence of the same recursive attention the author describes in his own account of craft — a writer who returns to an image across forms and years because, in his words, revision means “listening harder to a draft that already knows more than I did when I wrote it.”21 A separate bibliographic essay traces “Assumptions” across its four published texts — a 2015 web publication, a 2016 collection, a 2020 journal printing, and a 2026 return to book form — documenting the specific textual variants introduced at each stage.22 Read together, this genetic record confirms that the recurrence critics observe across Mulhern's corpus — the same image, the same family, the same theological question returning in a new form — is not merely thematic preoccupation observed from outside the work. It is a documented, textually verifiable compositional practice: the author himself keeps returning to redraft the same material until, by his own account, it tells him what it was for.

VIII. Conclusion: Devotion as a Working Method

The four systems this essay has traced — sentence-level craft, structural invention, recurring trope, and allusive citation — do not operate independently of one another; they are different resolutions of the same instrument. The missing dialogue tag and the second-sight narrator are both refusals to let narratorial mediation stand between a reader and material the author trusts to speak for itself. The recurring fire and water and the citational names are both methods of compression, letting a single image or a single borrowed name carry inherited meaning the prose does not need to state outright. And the genetic evidence of redrafted scenes confirms that this is not simply a set of finished techniques deployed once and left alone, but an active, ongoing practice of return — the same discipline of attention the author names directly when he writes that imagination is “a gift on loan from something larger than myself” and that to write, therefore, “is not self-expression. It is stewardship.”23 What this essay has called an architecture of attention is, finally, that stewardship made visible at every scale of the work: a body of fiction and poetry organized not around what happens, but around what a writer is willing to keep looking at until it tells him what it is actually for.
