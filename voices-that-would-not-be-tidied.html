A Critical Essay · On Dialect and Voice

The Voices That Would Not Be Tidied

Why an unfixed “Toreau” carries more truth than a corrected Thoreau — dialect as inheritance, and grammar as a map of who holds institutional power.

“‘Goodness is the only investment that never fails.’ A smart man by the name of Toreau said that.” — Aiden’s grandfather, in “Blindfolded”

I. The Grammar of Class: What Gets Corrected and What Does Not

The first thing a careful reading of Mulhern’s dialogue reveals is how selectively “incorrect” English is distributed across the social hierarchy of each story, and how precisely that distribution maps onto education, class, and institutional proximity. Catherine Mulroy, an Irish immigrant who arrived in Boston in the late 1930s and became a licensed practical nurse without benefit of formal secondary education, speaks in a register saturated with nonstandard contractions, elided consonants, and inherited idiom: “Will ya have a cup of tea?”; “Ya know what I think?”; “You gotta toughen up, Aiden”; “It’s hotter than a pig’s ass out there.” Her husband’s ghost, characterized explicitly as “an autodidact” who “never went to college” because “he couldn’t afford it and wasn’t allowed admission because he was an Irish immigrant,” speaks with the same phonetic markers layered over a stratum of half-absorbed, half-garbled book learning: “’Goodness is the only investment that never fails.’ A smart man by the name of Toreau said that,” and, elsewhere, the dialectal collapse of “th” into “t” that the narration explicitly names as a shared family trait — “Like my grandfather, her ‘th’ often sounded like ‘t’ or ‘d.’”

Contrast this with Nurse Nancy, a credentialed hospital employee of approximately the same generation as Aiden’s mother, whose speech is uniformly standard, slightly stilted, entirely without regional markers: “I want to do things right”; “Let me get in touch with the psychiatrist on call.” The contrast is not accidental or merely realistic; it is doing sociological work. Nancy’s grammatical correctness signals her institutional authority and, simultaneously, her outsider status relative to the family’s interior world — she is, in Catherine’s blunt formulation, “not new to hearing,” a woman whose polished diction marks her as someone who follows rules rather than someone who has lived hard enough to have earned the right to break them. The novel’s satire of Nancy is gentle, but it consistently associates her grammatical correctness with a kind of interpretive blindness: she “didn’t even know” her own patient’s name, memorizing “that board” rather than knowing the person.

This mapping of dialect onto authority recurs with remarkable consistency. The priest at Mission Church, an authority figure whose Latinate, liturgical register (“Beloved: See what love the Father has bestowed on us that we may be called the children of God”) is comically impenetrable to Catherine — “I have no idea what the hell he’s talking about. Sounds like a bunch of palaver” — represents institutional, educated language at its most alienating, a language the family can navigate strategically (well enough to con him) but never inhabit as their own. The policeman who stops Catherine for running a red light speaks in flat, procedural, standard English (“Ma’am, you ran a red light. . . . Consider this a warning”) that grants him legal authority precisely because it carries no trace of the vernacular that would mark him as one of the family’s own community, subject to its unspoken codes of mutual forgiveness. Standard English, throughout this fiction, is the language of institutions that can hurt you — hospitals, courts, churches speaking in their official register — while the vernacular is the language of the kitchen table, where mercy, complicity, and love actually get transacted.

II. The Malaprop as Inherited Wisdom, Not Ignorance

The most sophisticated dimension of Mulhern’s dialect work is his handling of malaprop and mispronunciation — errors that, examined closely, are never simply comic markers of ignorance but are instead evidence of an oral rather than textual relationship to knowledge, a distinction with real sociolinguistic and, ultimately, moral weight in these stories. When Aiden’s grandfather cites “Toreau” instead of Thoreau, or when Catherine misremembers the geography of Scripture as “Rephidim and Kadesh” rendered by her grandson as “Repapah” and “Kadiddle,” the text is documenting something more specific than a laugh line: it is documenting how wisdom moves through a culture that has been systematically denied access to the institutions — universities, seminaries, the world of the printed and footnoted book — that would have taught these citations “correctly.” Catherine’s husband absorbed Thoreau’s aphorism about goodness being an investment that never fails not from a syllabus but from some oral chain of transmission the novel never fully specifies — a borrowed book, a barroom conversation, a half-remembered lecture — and the mispronunciation of the philosopher’s name is the visible fingerprint of that alternate, unauthorized route of transmission. The wisdom arrived intact; the footnote did not.

This has a direct bearing on how the fiction treats questions of authority and credibility, questions explored at length elsewhere in relation to psychiatric diagnosis and second sight, but which apply with equal force to language itself: Mulhern’s fiction consistently grants real interpretive and moral authority to characters whose grammar marks them, by the standards of any institution, as uneducated, while consistently undercutting the moral authority of characters whose grammar marks them as credentialed. The grandfather’s imprecise Thoreau is not treated by the narrative as a lesser or corrupted version of the “real” Thoreau available to a university-trained reader; it is treated, in the text’s own moral economy, as more urgently true than most of what the credentialed authorities in these stories say, because it is invoked, correctly if not accurately, at exactly the moment a boy needs it — as an argument for returning a stolen chalice, as a code of ethics improvised under pressure. The malaprop, in other words, is not an obstacle between the character and the wisdom. It is the wisdom’s native accent.

III. The Author’s Own Account and Its Limits

It is worth pausing on the author’s own stated commitment — “I have proofread the narration in this book. I have not touched a single line of dialogue. That is a promise I make to the people who lent me their voices, most of whom are dead now, and none of whom needed to be improved” — because it clarifies the stakes this essay is trying to name, even as an outside critical reading should be cautious about simply accepting an author’s self-description of his own project as adequate literary analysis. Taken at face value, the claim positions dialect preservation as an act of filial and historical fidelity: these are not invented voices calibrated for literary effect but transcribed voices, remembered rather than composed, owed a debt of non-improvement. Whether or not every line of dialogue in Mulhern’s published fiction is in fact drawn verbatim from remembered speech — a claim no outside reader is in a position to verify, and which this essay does not attempt to adjudicate — the effect on the page is consistent with that stated intention: an unusual tonal steadiness in the nonstandard usages, a refusal to let any character’s dialect slip, soften, or “clean up” for the convenience of a scene, even in moments of high emotional stakes where a less disciplined writer might smooth a grandmother’s grammar to make her grief more legible to a general reader. Catherine’s most devastating confession in the entire cycle — “Your grandfather and I had been arguing. He was drunk again. I just left him in the chair. I should have made him come with me” — is delivered in grammar only slightly more relaxed than her comic register elsewhere; the pain does not purchase eloquence the character has not earned, and the prose is stricter about maintaining that consistency than many comparable works of literary fiction tend to be.

IV. Sound as Class Marker Across Ethnic Registers

Reading Molly Bonamici alongside the Aiden stories reveals that Mulhern’s sociolinguistic method is portable across ethnic community without becoming generic — that is, the Italian-American register of Nonna’s household is built from its own distinct set of markers rather than simply recycling the Irish-Catholic dialect wholesale with different proper nouns substituted in. Where Catherine’s idiom draws on Irish-inflected constructions, Catholic liturgical vocabulary repurposed for domestic use (“tribulation,” “venial sin,” “God will understand”), and inherited proverbs specifically identified as sayings “from the old country,” the Italian-American register Mulhern builds elsewhere in his corpus draws on a different set of resources — different terms of endearment, a different relationship to food and family obligation, a different set of inherited proverbs. That the underlying sociolinguistic mechanism — dialect as inherited property, malaprop as evidence of oral rather than institutional transmission, grammatical correctness correlated with institutional and often untrustworthy authority — recurs across both ethnic registers, without either register simply repeating the other’s specific vocabulary, suggests that Mulhern has developed something closer to a general theory of working-class immigrant speech in American fiction than a single dialect he happens to know well from personal history. The theory, distilled, might run: the vernacular a family speaks at its own kitchen table is a technology of trust, deployed nowhere else, understood fully by no institution, and lost the moment it is corrected into something a stranger could grade.

V. What Is Lost When Dialogue Is “Improved”

This essay’s final claim concerns what is actually at stake, thematically rather than merely stylistically, in Mulhern’s refusal to standardize. Consider what would be lost if an editor, in the name of readability, “corrected” Catherine’s most quoted line in the entire corpus — “’Goodness is the only investment that never fails.’ A smart man by the name of Toreau said that” — into grammatically pristine prose: “A wise man named Henry David Thoreau wrote that goodness is an investment that never fails.” The corrected sentence loses, in a single stroke, the specificity of oral transmission (the vague, hedging “a smart man by the name of”), the phonetic evidence of the speaker’s own linguistic history (the mispronunciation), and the intimate, unbookish relationship to the quotation that makes the grandfather’s citation of Thoreau moving rather than merely erudite. The correction would not simply smooth a sentence. It would erase the evidence of exactly how, and at what cost, this family came to possess the wisdom it possesses — evidence the “incorrect” version preserves precisely because it has not been tidied into a form a stranger, or an institution, would find more presentable.

This is, finally, why the essay’s title borrows Mulhern’s own vocabulary of refusal rather than converting it into more conventional literary-critical language: these are, quite literally, in the fiction’s own moral accounting, voices that would not be tidied — voices belonging mostly to the dead, transmitted through grandchildren who do not fully understand what they have inherited until, like the narrators of these stories, they are old enough to write it down, misspellings and all, because the misspellings were never the point being missed. They were the point being made.
