A Welcome

For readers, writers, and students of the craft

James Mulhern is a Philadelphia-based novelist, poet, essayist, and professor emeritus of English whose novels, short stories, poetry, and nonfiction have appeared in international literary journals and anthologies more than three hundred times. His novel Give Them Unquiet Dreams received a Kirkus starred review and was named one of the Best Books of 2019.

He was awarded a fully paid Creative Writing Fellowship to the University of Oxford, nominated for a Pushcart Prize, shortlisted for the Aesthetica Creative Writing Award, longlisted for the Fish Short Story Prize, and named a Finalist for the Tuscany Prize in Catholic Fiction. His novels have earned favorable critiques from Kirkus Reviews, awards from Readers' Favorite Book Awards, and Red Ribbon recognition from the United Kingdom's Wishing Shelf Book Awards.

He is the founder of Silver Current Press, a Philadelphia imprint devoted to literary fiction, poetry, and the art of the well-made book. After three decades in the classroom, he now writes and publishes full time.

In the Tradition Of

Literary Lineage

Mulhern's work has drawn comparison to writers across the English-language tradition — from the great Irish short-story masters to the earliest novelists of the form.

Flannery O'Connor
"Influenced by writers such as Flannery O'Connor, James Joyce, and Edgar Allan Poe, Mulhern renders slices of Boston and South Florida life with comedy, horror, and moments of grace." — Editorial description, Barnes & Noble

Mulhern evokes O'Connor in the way he lets grace arrive through violence and the grotesque, never through sentiment. Like her, he trusts that a single startling gesture — a cruelty, an accident, an unbidden act of mercy — can crack a character open and let the light in. His Irish-Catholic households, like her rural Georgia ones, are theaters of fallen people awaiting a revelation they neither expect nor deserve, and his comedy, like hers, is the dark, deadpan kind that makes the eventual tenderness land all the harder.
James Joyce
The Joycean inheritance: Irish-Catholic memory rendered as moral architecture, time folded against itself, and an ear for the music of vernacular speech.

Mulhern evokes Joyce in his fidelity to the ordinary epiphany — the small domestic moment that quietly turns luminous, as in the closing pages of Dubliners. He shares Joyce's conviction that a city and a childhood can be reconstructed sentence by sentence from the cadences of remembered speech, and that paralysis, exile, and homecoming are the permanent weather of the Irish imagination. The kitchens and parlors of his Boston carry the same charged stillness as the rooms of Joyce's Dublin.
The Irish Masters
"All of the storytelling prowess of the contemporary Irish masters" — Readers' Favorite. Compared in tradition to William Trevor, John McGahern, and Colm Tóibín for patient, character-driven prose and quiet moral seriousness.

Mulhern evokes Trevor, McGahern, and Tóibín in his patience — his willingness to let a story accumulate meaning slowly, through gesture and silence rather than incident. Like McGahern, he honors the textures of rural and parish life and the long shadow of the family; like Trevor, he extends compassion even to his most compromised characters; like Tóibín, he writes restraint as a form of feeling, trusting the reader to hear what is left unsaid.
Edgar Allan Poe
The thread of the gothic — paranormal seasoning grounded in psychological realism — connects Give Them Unquiet Dreams to a long American tradition of haunted houses and haunted families.

Mulhern evokes Poe in his understanding that the truly frightening thing is rarely the ghost but the guilty, grieving mind that conjures it. Like Poe, he uses the apparatus of the uncanny — the visitation, the omen, the house that will not rest — as a way of dramatizing buried memory and inherited dread. The supernatural in his fiction is never decorative; it is the return of what a family has tried, and failed, to forget.
Daniel Defoe
The title character of Molly Bonamici takes her name and her resilient spirit from Defoe's Moll Flanders — a strong female protagonist who, in Mulhern's words, "respects the integrity of her own mind."

Mulhern evokes Defoe in his appetite for the resourceful survivor — the figure who improvises a self against poverty, circumstance, and respectable opinion. Like Defoe, he grants his heroine a frank, first-person vitality and an unsentimental will to endure, letting her tell her own story in her own unapologetic voice. The result is a character study in the oldest tradition of the English novel: a life rendered whole, flaws and cunning and dignity alike.
Thoreau & Yeats
Kirkus noted that Mulhern's prose is "elevated by a liberal use of quotes and sayings ranging from Bible verses to Thoreau and Yeats" — placing his sentences in conversation with the moral and lyric traditions of both.

Mulhern evokes Thoreau in his insistence that a life be examined and lived deliberately, and that the integrity of the individual mind is the first principle of any worthwhile art. He evokes Yeats in his lyric reach — the readiness to set the homely particulars of family and place against the larger questions of time, memory, and the soul. Together they mark the two poles of his sensibility: the moral plainness of the American transcendentalist and the symbolic music of the Irish poet.
