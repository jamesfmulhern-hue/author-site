An Essay · On the Novel

Pastiche and Undertow

How Molly Bonamici works with Dante, Defoe, Poe, Shakespeare, Fitzgerald, Dickinson, and Kate Chopin — with new attention to naming, domestic iconography, and altered doubles.

“Open your mind to what I shall disclose, and hold it fast within you; / he who hears, but does not hold what he has heard, learns nothing.” — Dante Alighieri, quoted as the epigraph to Molly Bonamici

Molly Bonamici announces a literary inheritance before its first chapter begins. The epigraphs pair Hamlet with Dante’s Paradiso, and the novel returns to both questions: what exceeds an orderly philosophy, and what a reader is willing to hold. The Dantean material is real and recurrent — the policeman called Virgil, the final cab driver Bea, short for Beatrice, and a last movement toward sea, light, and home — but it works as a framework inside a realist, comic, often violent life rather than as a one-to-one reproduction of the Comedy.

Molly’s journey can accordingly be read through descent, difficult reckoning, and a final opening toward possibility. Her language of dark woods and a steep ascent makes the Dantean echo available; the novel’s ending returns it to lived time, grief, bodily memory, and an unornamented walk out of the water. The value of the pattern is not that it makes Molly’s crimes harmless or assures a tidy conversion. It establishes an imaginative measure against which the narrator’s coldness, skepticism, and eventual receptivity can be read.

The new work on names changes the first page of that reading. “Molly” is a nickname joining Margaret and Mary, so the protagonist carries Mrs. Muldoon’s Margaret Mary Alacoque association from the outset. The final scene takes place on the feast of Saint Margaret Mary. What seemed at first a familiar diminutive turns out to be an inherited devotional identity, disclosed only after the novel has made a subject of names, nicknames, and the authority to give them.

“Molly” does not stand alone in this respect; it belongs to a long lineage of names that descend, by one route or another, from Mary itself — ultimately the Hebrew Miriam, a name of disputed but suggestive meaning, variously glossed as “beloved,” “bitter,” or “rebellious.” Moll and Mollie are the same diminutive in slightly different dress, historically interchangeable with Polly and Poll, both once common English nicknames for Mary. Maureen comes down through the Irish Máirín, itself a diminutive of Máire, the Irish form of Mary — and Mareen is generally traced to that same Irish root, though a few sources connect it instead to Marina or the Scandinavian Maren, a reminder that these names braided together across languages rather than descending in one clean line. Myra is a more curious case: coined by the Elizabethan poet Fulke Greville, it is widely read as an anagram of Mary, and later usage often let it stand as a variant of Mary or of Moira, the Irish form of the same name. Maria, Marie, Marion, Mariam, Maren, Mara, and the Hebrew Miryam fill out the family further, along with domestic English forms like Mamie, Mame, and Mayme. That so many women, across so many centuries and languages, have carried some version of the same name is itself a kind of argument: a name held in trust and passed hand to hand, the way devotion is passed hand to hand, long after its bearers have been forgotten by everyone but the family that kept saying it.

Miriam is not only the etymological root but a biblical figure in her own right, and the novel reaches back to her directly rather than only through Mary. In the Hebrew Bible, Miriam is the prophetess who leads the Israelites in song after the crossing of the Red Sea and who watches over the infant Moses on the Nile (Exodus 2, 15:20) — the older sister who keeps faith while a helpless child is carried toward deliverance. The novel’s final chapter draws on that inheritance quietly: two sisters are named Martha and Miriam, the same pairing as the Gospels’ best-known sisters, Martha and Mary of Bethany (Luke 10:38–42), with Miriam standing in as the Hebrew original of that second sister’s name. The chapter layers still more scripture beneath the scene without naming any of it — a child spontaneously repeating the parable of the lost lamb (Luke 15:3–7), a cab driver wearing an earring patterned after the sun, a crescent moon, and twin stars (echoing the woman “clothed with the sun” of Revelation 12:1), and Venus rising beside stargazer lilies just before Molly’s baptismal swim, a funerary flower and a planet of desire converging at the moment of a conversion the book never once calls by that name. The effect is cumulative rather than declarative: the novel does not argue for belief, it simply keeps setting belief’s furniture down in the room until the reader notices how much of it is there.

I dedicated the book that grew out of this novel to Mary — the Blessed Mother — out of love for my grandmother, who revered her, and out of my own belief that the Blessed Mother represents strength, love that endures without complaint, and the capacity to face hardship without being broken by it. Mary has too rarely been given the credit she deserves within the Church that reveres her: a strong woman, and a woman of love that was enduring and unquestioned. That omission belongs to a larger pattern, one this novel is also concerned with — women’s contributions, foundational to families and to culture, going unacknowledged even as they hold everything together. Naming Molly into that lineage of Marys, knowingly or not, was a way of paying the same respect: to my grandmother, and to every woman whose strength is assumed rather than named.

“Bonamici” means “good friends” in Italian. The name’s irony belongs to a novel in which loyalty, information, family obligation, and violence remain inseparable. It is more productive to keep the irony at that scale than to make it a single allusion-key: the name measures the distance between a promise of fellowship and the damaged relations through which Molly moves.

Defoe, Poe, Fitzgerald, Dickinson, Chopin, and Shakespeare remain part of the novel’s visible conversation. The Defoe parallel places a female rogue inside a confessional first-person tradition; Fitzgerald’s green light is explicitly revised as Molly’s demand for authenticity; Dickinson supplies both Nonna’s instruction to tell truth “slant” and the final vocabulary of possibility. The closing swim closely reworks The Awakening, retaining its sensory pressure and solitary movement into water while reversing its terminal direction: Molly rises and walks back to shore.

Poe’s presence runs deeper than gothic atmosphere. “Bonamici” is the same ironic device as Poe’s Fortunato in “The Cask of Amontillado” — a name that means good fortune, or good friends, hung over a betrayal — and Felicity, poisoned by Molly, carries the same reversed logic: a name meaning happiness borne by a girl who does not survive to earn it, murdered by a narrator who confesses she cannot feel happiness at all. The allusion surfaces again, more directly, when Molly’s freshman roommate Ashley dies after eating a cookie Molly’s grandmother has cursed by letter; Molly checks for a pulse and finds none, and the scene’s claustrophobic dormitory setting, its detached mannequin hand flung across the room, and Molly’s own clinical calm in the face of a death she privately feels complicit in all rework the guilt-haunted proximity to a body that drives “The Tell-Tale Heart.” Later, at Mrs. Muldoon’s death, Molly recites Poe’s own language of the curtain and the funeral pall — allusion surfacing as direct quotation rather than structural echo. Shakespeare, beyond the Hamlet epigraph, supplies Molly’s sardonic register throughout: “methinks she doth protest too much” sits alongside Measure for Measure’s “to die, and go we know not where” and Macbeth’s “tale told by an idiot,” giving a narrator capable of real cruelty a wit that keeps her readable rather than merely cold.

James Joyce enters through Gabe rather than through direct quotation. The Christmas visit to Gabe’s mother recreates the architecture of “The Dead” — an older matriarch presiding over a holiday gathering, a companion named for Joyce’s own Gabriel, and a quiet confrontation with mortality at a moment meant for warmth. Since Molly has already been told she is an “Angel of Death,” the scene becomes a small, knowing joke at Joyce’s expense as much as an homage: the living and the dead are shadowing each other again, but the character carrying that shadow this time knows it. The prose itself occasionally admits the debt outright — a late passage watching “two planes… one ascending, one descending, a metaphor for life” while Molly’s “soul swooned” borrows Joyce’s cadence along with his subject.

The Great Gatsby supplies a second death scene, not only the green light. Myra Bocca, a grifter run down by a car, is named as a near-anagram and auditory match for Myrtle Wilson, Fitzgerald’s victim of the same reckless carelessness; the detail of a yellow car doing the killing imports Fitzgerald’s association of that color with counterfeit wealth directly into a Florida strip-mall setting. Flannery O’Connor supplies a second grandmother problem: Nonna’s superstition, her manipulations dressed as piety, and her role in setting Ashley’s death in motion all descend from the grandmother of “A Good Man Is Hard to Find,” whose own hypocrisy and big mouth get her family killed. Molly’s eventual refusal of the “Angel of Death” role she has been handed is the novel’s answer to the Misfit’s theology that people behave decently only under threat of death: she declines both the grandmother’s hypocrisy and the Misfit’s fatalism, and finds her release in water instead of violence.

The new concordance also makes the novel’s quieter preparation easier to see. A gecko watches the work’s one sustained theological dispute; a cardinal arrives as Gabe asks for a sign; and a Bleeding Heart vine droops and revives with Molly’s changing state before the Sacred Heart is named. These are not arguments by themselves. They give the final chapter a prepared visual vocabulary of animal witness, sign, wound, and renewal.

A therapist’s office supplies a smaller, quieter gallery of allusion: Munch’s The Scream and Van Gogh’s Starry Night hang on the wall where Molly is asked to name what she feels, one canvas externalizing the panic she keeps flat in her own narration, the other offering a turbulent, spiritually charged sky as its unspoken alternative. Socrates’s “I know that I know nothing” does similar work in the same scene, standing in for a confession Molly cannot yet make in religious language. None of these three is argued into the text; they furnish it, the way the gecko and the cardinal do, so that a reader who notices them finds confirmation rather than instruction.

The novel’s most tender allusion is musical rather than literary, and it belongs to Mrs. Muldoon rather than to Molly. Sober and gentle for once at Molly’s graduation party, Mrs. Muldoon sings “Moon River” to her — “my huckleberry friend” — and dies within weeks. A hundred pages of comic exasperation with this drunken, unreliable widow are spent so that the song lands as the moral center of the book rather than as sentiment. In the closing pages, Molly gives a watch and a Sacred Heart locket — the same devotional emblem the Bleeding Heart vine has been rehearsing all along — to two Jewish girls on a plane, a small, unforced gift that carries Catholic iconography across a religious line without asking anyone to convert. It is the same generosity the novel keeps extending to Mary herself: devotion offered freely, without argument, to whoever is in the seat beside you.

The women who recur around Molly should not be treated as hidden instances of one unchanged grandmother. The books work through altered doubles: similar household roles, phrases, gestures, and moral mixtures recur, but each reappears under new pressure. The resemblance between Nonna and Nana is therefore a form of composition, not a license to collapse two novels into a single continuous biography.

A full census of the novel’s proper names settles a question the doubled name raises. Mulhern does not run a moral code through his naming: sacred names are not reserved for the devout, nor secular ones for the cruel. He loads a small set of hinge-names, usually against their bearers, and leaves the walk-ons semantically ordinary. Agnella carries the Italian and Latin sense of lamb, chosen by a woman it does not describe. Felicity is the Latin virtue-name for happiness, set on a life short of it. Gabriel, the messenger, belongs to a former priest. Hulga arrives straight out of Flannery O’Connor, and Dr. Diver out of Fitzgerald. The restraint is the point: names carry meaning here only where the novel wants pressure applied.

The same restraint governs the narrator’s voice. Molly does not become scriptural at sacred moments, and it is worth saying so plainly, because a religious reading would prefer that she did. Her domestic prose alternates between sensuous catalogue and flat inventory; violence contracts into terse Anglo-Saxon; death expands into clinical, Latinate observation; the funeral Mass turns formal and hypotactic because she is examining its claim rather than assenting to it. Even the sea, the novel’s most charged element, is rendered comparatively plainly, with a single personification in place of biblical parallelism. What actually raises her diction is interpretation itself — the moment she consciously reads an event, whether through a rite, a literary memory, or water. Belief, in this novel, is a late act of reading rather than a settled register.

Read together — the Dantean frame, the doubled name, the Italian surname, the Poe irony, the Joyce and O’Connor grandmothers, the Gatsby deaths, and the novel’s overt literary debts — these make a layered pastiche rather than a puzzle with one answer. The book uses inherited texts to make judgment harder, not easier. Its final possibility is earned neither by innocence nor by a doctrinal resolution, but by a narrator learning that the language she has dismissed may still describe something she can receive.
