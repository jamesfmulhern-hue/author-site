A Scholarly Essay · On the Novel

Pastiche and Undertow

How Molly Bonamici is built on the architecture of Dante’s Divine Comedy — and answers Defoe, Poe, Shakespeare, Fitzgerald, Dickinson, and Kate Chopin along the way.

“Open your mind to what I shall disclose, and hold it fast within you; / he who hears, but does not hold what he has heard, learns nothing.” — Dante Alighieri, quoted as the epigraph to Molly Bonamici

Molly Bonamici announces its debt to the literary tradition before its first chapter begins. Its epigraphs pair Shakespeare’s Hamlet (“There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, / Than are dreamt of in your philosophy”) with Dante’s Paradiso (“Open your mind to what I shall disclose, and hold it fast within you”). Read as mere ornament, the pairing looks like taste. Read closely, it is a blueprint: the entire novel is built on the architecture of the Divine Comedy, and once that structure is seen, it reorganizes everything else the book is doing — its coldness, its comedy, its crimes, and its final, unsentimental grace.

Molly is the pilgrim, and the narrative is her journey through the three realms of the afterlife — understood, in the novel’s own theology of the eternal present, as three conditions of one earthly life. The Inferno is the long middle of the book: the years of what Molly calls her “sexual lawlessness,” the deaths that surround her, the moral cold in which she is frozen like one of Dante’s damned — fittingly, since her defining trait is an inability to feel, the very sin Dante punishes in the ice of his lowest circle. The descent begins, as Dante’s does, with a loss of the way: standing at her parents’ grave, Molly finds herself “lost in dark woods… the straight path I had envisioned for my life disappeared.” The Purgatorio is the ascent she names directly — “my own purgatory,” a “steep hill that grew more precipitous the further I ascended” — the slow, painful work of self-reckoning. And the Paradiso is the ending: the awakening, the swim, the return to the water, and the fulfillment of the Dante epigraph itself, as a lifelong unbeliever is granted her vision of the light and walks from the sea “grateful to be home at last.”

The guides are placed with the same deliberateness. At the very gravesite where Molly enters her dark wood, a policeman introduces himself: “Call me Virgil” — a name Molly herself pauses over as “strange,” a flag planted for the reader who is paying attention. The true guide, however, is Gabe (Gabriel) Callaghan, an ex-priest who leads Molly, Virgil-like, through the underworld of her adult life. Older women in the novel — Nonna, and above all the dying Mrs. Muldoon — function as Dante’s sibyls, offering Molly, under cover of gossip and card tricks, a doctrine of grace she is not yet ready to receive. And the Bible her mother gives her, which she scorns at the time as “a silly gift,” is the Paradiso’s promised disclosure held in reserve, its meaning arriving only at the summit, when Molly is at last able to read it. This is why the book’s apparent coldness is not a flaw but a beginning: the Commedia must start in hell, and the reader who recognizes the shape has known since the epigraph that Molly is not a case study but a pilgrim, and that the destination was always the light.

If the vertical structure of Molly Bonamici is Dante’s ascent, its horizontal genre — the shape of the life story itself — descends from Daniel Defoe’s Moll Flanders (1722), the founding English picaresque of the female rogue, and the echo begins with the name. “Molly” is “Moll” — both diminutives of Mary, both borne by a candid, unrepentant, first-person heroine who survives a world stacked against women by stealing, scheming, and lying beautifully. Both books are frank criminal confessions that refuse to let the reader simply condemn their heroine, and both end in a version of redemption: Defoe’s Moll dies “penitent”; Mulhern’s Molly awakens. The picaresque arc of the female rogue is bent, in each case, toward grace — which is why the same life can be told, at once, as a rogue’s tale and as a pilgrimage.

Edgar Allan Poe supplies the novel’s gothic irony, and it is hidden in the protagonist’s own name. “Bonamici” means “good friends” — a fact the text makes sure the reader notices, both through a policeman (“Your last name means ‘good friends,’”) and through Molly herself, who reflects, “I have always found our surname ironic.” This is precisely Poe’s technique in “The Cask of Amontillado,” where the victim is named Fortunato — “the fortunate one” — lured to his death by a false friend who calls him “my friend” while walling him up alive. Molly, a poisoner and survivor whose name proclaims friendship, is Fortunato’s ironic descendant. The irony compounds in the name of the girl Molly poisons: Felicity, meaning happiness, and, in the theological register the novel invokes throughout, beatitude — the blessedness of the saved. A character named Happiness is murdered by a narrator who has confessed she cannot feel happiness at all (“Sometimes I don’t feel anything at all”), and, in the Dantean frame, felicity is beatitude itself — meaning Molly, the pilgrim, must annihilate a false, counterfeit felicity long before she can earn the true one in her final, awakening swim.

Shakespeare, meanwhile, supplies the sardonic register that keeps Molly’s coldness readable as wit rather than mere cruelty. Beyond the Hamlet epigraph, the play threads through her murderous cool — “Methinks she doth protest too much” — alongside Measure for Measure’s meditation on death (“Ay, but to die, and go we know not where”) and Macbeth’s “tale told by an idiot.” Shakespeare gives the sociopath-narrator her wit and her metaphysics at once, so that her cruelty and her intelligence never separate.

The novel’s most explicit and most argumentative pastiche, though, is its running quarrel with The Great Gatsby, assigned reading in Molly’s own adolescence. She rejects the book on first encounter — “Daisy was a pain in the ass, and Gatsby was anything but great” — even as she unknowingly repurposes its central image for her own longing: “unlike the dog’s lure, my lure, my green light, would be real — to be as authentic as possible, regardless of what people thought.” Fitzgerald’s green light, transplanted from Gatsby’s romantic self-delusion to Molly’s demand for authenticity, converts homage into correction: where Gatsby’s light represents a fantasy that destroys him, Molly’s version of the same image is offered as its opposite, a lure toward selfhood rather than away from it. The novel returns to Gatsby a second time through Gabe, discovered rereading it years later alongside Hamlet — a quiet formal echo of the opening epigraphs, suggesting that the novel’s characters remain in ongoing conversation with the canon rather than settling their relationship to it once, in youth.

Emily Dickinson frames the novel at both ends. Her line “Tell all the truth but tell it slant” is quoted by Nonna as literal instruction in the family art of the productive half-lie — allusion functioning as characterization, the poems a character reads revealing who she is. And the novel closes by earning its Dickinson: “I dwell in Possibility – / A fairer House than Prose… The spreading wide my narrow Hands / To gather Paradise –” A narrator who has lived her whole life imprisoned in prose — in calculation, in the literal — finally chooses Possibility, and the novel’s frame closes precisely as it opened, with an unbeliever brought, by increments, into a poem.

That closing swim is also a deliberate rewriting of the most famous ending in American women’s fiction: the death of Edna Pontellier in Kate Chopin’s The Awakening (1899). The sensory signature is reproduced almost exactly. Chopin closes on “the hum of bees, and the musky odor of pinks”; Mulhern gives “a faint hum, like bees, and the smell of stargazer lilies.” Chopin’s refrain is “the touch of the sea is sensuous, enfolding the body in its soft, close embrace”; Mulhern gives “the water’s soft, close embrace.” Both heroines strip, swim out alone toward the horizon, and are associated with Venus rising from the sea — and the intelligent reader is meant to recognize the source, then feel the reversal. For Chopin, the swim is a death: Edna, defeated by a world with no room for her awakened self, swims out and does not return. For Mulhern, the identical swim is a resurrection: Molly rises from the same water and “walk[s] onto the beach, grateful to be home at last.” Where Chopin’s awakening ends in drowning, Mulhern’s ends in a homecoming.

The awakening itself surfaces earlier than the final page, and Mulhern signals it through allusion rather than plot. Reading Thoreau on regret (“To regret deeply is to live afresh”) and feeling, in Dickinson’s exact phrase, “an ‘internal difference,’” Molly resolves to “live afresh” — borrowed words marking the first crack in a lifelong unbelief. By the final chapter, floating on her back beneath “a scintillating blue” sky and recognizing that a lifetime of small kindnesses from Mrs. Muldoon “were acts of pure love,” the self-declared unbeliever has, without announcing it, arrived at a fully Transcendentalist vision: “Venus sparkled high above the Earth. Believing in possibility, I spread my arms wide and swam slowly to the shore… I rose and walked onto the beach, grateful to be home at last.” It is not a plot reversal so much as the surfacing, in a resistant narrator’s own language, of the belief the whole book has withheld.

Working beneath all of this is the subtext built from Molly’s extended Italian-American Catholic family — Nonna, her aunts Helena and Bianca — whose domestic authority and inherited religious vocabulary (feast days, saints, the language of sacrifice and guilt) recall the same inherited Catholic grammar that structures Mulhern’s poetry and his story “Assumptions.” Nonna is, in fact, the same larcenous, loving grandmother who appears as Nana in Give Them Unquiet Dreams under a different name — the clearest sign that Molly Bonamici is not a separate book so much as another room in the same house, its literary debts and its family inheritance both part of one continuous design.

Read together — the Dantean architecture, the Defoe genre, the Poe irony, the Shakespeare wit, the Fitzgerald quarrel, the Dickinson frame, and the Chopin reversal — these debts form a layered pastiche in the oldest and most honest sense of the word: not concealment, as plagiarism is, nor mockery, as parody can be, but homage, a debt acknowledged rather than hidden, offered to the reader attentive enough to receive it. A fuller treatment of this reading, alongside the same architecture traced across Mulhern’s other novels, stories, and poems, appears in the critical study The Writing Style of James Mulhern (Silver Current Press).
