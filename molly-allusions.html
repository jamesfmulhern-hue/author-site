A Scholarly Essay · On the Novel

Pastiche and Undertow

How Molly Bonamici works with Dante, Defoe, Poe, Shakespeare, Fitzgerald, Dickinson, and Kate Chopin — with new attention to naming, domestic iconography, and altered doubles.

“Open your mind to what I shall disclose, and hold it fast within you; / he who hears, but does not hold what he has heard, learns nothing.” — Dante Alighieri, quoted as the epigraph to Molly Bonamici

Molly Bonamici announces a literary inheritance before its first chapter begins. The epigraphs pair Hamlet with Dante’s Paradiso, and the novel returns to both questions: what exceeds an orderly philosophy, and what a reader is willing to hold. The Dantean material is real and recurrent — the policeman called Virgil, the final cab driver Bea, short for Beatrice, and a last movement toward sea, light, and home — but it works as a framework inside a realist, comic, often violent life rather than as a one-to-one reproduction of the Comedy.

Molly’s journey can accordingly be read through descent, difficult reckoning, and a final opening toward possibility. Her language of dark woods and a steep ascent makes the Dantean echo available; the novel’s ending returns it to lived time, grief, bodily memory, and an unornamented walk out of the water. The value of the pattern is not that it makes Molly’s crimes harmless or assures a tidy conversion. It establishes an imaginative measure against which the narrator’s coldness, skepticism, and eventual receptivity can be read.

The new work on names changes the first page of that reading. “Molly” is a nickname joining Margaret and Mary, so the protagonist carries Mrs. Muldoon’s Margaret Mary Alacoque association from the outset. The final scene takes place on the feast of Saint Margaret Mary. What seemed at first a familiar diminutive turns out to be an inherited devotional identity, disclosed only after the novel has made a subject of names, nicknames, and the authority to give them.

“Bonamici” means “good friends” in Italian. The name’s irony belongs to a novel in which loyalty, information, family obligation, and violence remain inseparable. It is more productive to keep the irony at that scale than to make it a single allusion-key: the name measures the distance between a promise of fellowship and the damaged relations through which Molly moves.

Defoe, Poe, Fitzgerald, Dickinson, Chopin, and Shakespeare remain part of the novel’s visible conversation. The Defoe parallel places a female rogue inside a confessional first-person tradition; Fitzgerald’s green light is explicitly revised as Molly’s demand for authenticity; Dickinson supplies both Nonna’s instruction to tell truth “slant” and the final vocabulary of possibility. The closing swim closely reworks The Awakening, retaining its sensory pressure and solitary movement into water while reversing its terminal direction: Molly rises and walks back to shore.

The new concordance also makes the novel’s quieter preparation easier to see. A gecko watches the work’s one sustained theological dispute; a cardinal arrives as Gabe asks for a sign; and a Bleeding Heart vine droops and revives with Molly’s changing state before the Sacred Heart is named. These are not arguments by themselves. They give the final chapter a prepared visual vocabulary of animal witness, sign, wound, and renewal.

The women who recur around Molly should not be treated as hidden instances of one unchanged grandmother. The books work through altered doubles: similar household roles, phrases, gestures, and moral mixtures recur, but each reappears under new pressure. The resemblance between Nonna and Nana is therefore a form of composition, not a license to collapse two novels into a single continuous biography.

A full census of the novel’s proper names settles a question the doubled name raises. Mulhern does not run a moral code through his naming: sacred names are not reserved for the devout, nor secular ones for the cruel. He loads a small set of hinge-names, usually against their bearers, and leaves the walk-ons semantically ordinary. Agnella carries the Italian and Latin sense of lamb, chosen by a woman it does not describe. Felicity is the Latin virtue-name for happiness, set on a life short of it. Gabriel, the messenger, belongs to a former priest. Hulga arrives straight out of Flannery O’Connor, and Dr. Diver out of Fitzgerald. The restraint is the point: names carry meaning here only where the novel wants pressure applied.

The same restraint governs the narrator’s voice. Molly does not become scriptural at sacred moments, and it is worth saying so plainly, because a religious reading would prefer that she did. Her domestic prose alternates between sensuous catalogue and flat inventory; violence contracts into terse Anglo-Saxon; death expands into clinical, Latinate observation; the funeral Mass turns formal and hypotactic because she is examining its claim rather than assenting to it. Even the sea, the novel’s most charged element, is rendered comparatively plainly, with a single personification in place of biblical parallelism. What actually raises her diction is interpretation itself — the moment she consciously reads an event, whether through a rite, a literary memory, or water. Belief, in this novel, is a late act of reading rather than a settled register.

Read together, the Dantean frame, the doubled name, the Italian surname, and the novel’s overt literary debts make a layered pastiche rather than a puzzle with one answer. The book uses inherited texts to make judgment harder, not easier. Its final possibility is earned neither by innocence nor by a doctrinal resolution, but by a narrator learning that the language she has dismissed may still describe something she can receive.
