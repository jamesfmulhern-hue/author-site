Resemblances & Comparisons

Kindred Writers

The writers whose work most resembles James Mulhern’s — each set beside a line from his own poems and stories, with a note on what the comparison reveals.

In the course of close reading — and repeatedly in AI-assisted analysis through Perplexity — the same affinities kept surfacing: Mulhern’s work placed beside Flannery O’Connor, James Joyce, Raymond Carver, and others. This page gathers those resemblances, setting a line from each writer beside a line from Mulhern, so the kinship can be seen rather than merely asserted.

Resemblance is not equivalence. To be placed beside a great writer is not to be that writer; it is only to share, in some small way, a habit of attention. These affinities are offered as a reader’s observation, not as a claim of equal stature.

Flannery O’Connor

1925 – 1964

The comic-grotesque; the profane grandmother; grace delivered through violence.

Both writers place a grandmother at the hinge of comedy and horror. O’Connor’s grandmother is shot by the Misfit; Mulhern’s Nonna stirs poison into a dish with the same domestic tenderness with which she bakes. In both, the grotesque is not a distortion of the ordinary but its truest face — and grace, when it arrives, comes through the very violence that ought to exclude it.

James Joyce

1882 – 1941

The Irish epiphany; the everyday made luminous.

Both writers find the epiphany not in revelation but in the ordinary — a snowfall, a hand on a wall. Joyce’s snow falls on the living and the dead alike; Mulhern’s speaker touches the wall his great-uncle touched and watches him “move from darkness into light.” The Irish inheritance is the same: the dead are never quite gone, and the everyday is the only place the sacred can be found.

Raymond Carver

1938 – 1988

Minimalism; the flat report that carries the feeling.

Both writers trust understatement to do the work of grief. Carver’s baker offers bread as the only answer to a child’s death; Mulhern’s speaker records the hour he left the hospital without a word of feeling. The restraint is not coldness but measurement — the gap between the size of the wound and the size of the sentence is where the emotion is kept.

Seamus Heaney

1939 – 2013

Irish memory; the hand as the instrument of inheritance.

Heaney’s pen rests “snug as a gun” beside the spade his father dug with; the poem honors labor through a different instrument. Mulhern’s speaker keeps a photograph of his father’s hands on his shoulders — “one moment, framed” — as the evidence of a love that was never spoken. Both writers know that what passes between generations passes through the hands, and that memory is a physical act.

Emily Dickinson

1830 – 1886

The slant of light; the resonance left unpressed.

Dickinson’s “certain Slant of light” carries a weight it never explains; Mulhern’s grandfather’s “blue eyes lit by a slant of kitchen sun” does the same. Any reader who wishes to hear Dickinson behind the line is free to — and the poem neither invites nor forbids it. That tact is the shared inheritance: the resonance is placed within reach and then left alone.

T. S. Eliot

1888 – 1965

The objective correlative; grief held in objects.

Eliot’s formula names what Mulhern’s poems practice. The word grief never appears in the poems of Crossings; instead there is a robe folded on a table, a photograph propped by a casket, a print shattered on a floor. The emotion is not declared but reached for, through the specific object that has witnessed it. Eliot supplied the theory; the poems supply the proof.

Walt Whitman

1819 – 1892

The levelled catalogue; nothing too small to be kept.

Whitman’s catalogues give the same grammatical weight to the grand and the humble; Mulhern’s “To Be” places Chaucer’s Wife of Bath and a lost sock under a couch in the same syntactic rank. Both are acts of democratic attention — the conviction that nothing is too small to be kept, and that a list, honestly made, is a form of praise.

Alice Munro

1931 – 2024

The gap; the unspoken that carries the story.

Munro builds her stories out of what is left out; Mulhern’s poems do the same. After the grandmother explains why her toes are crooked, the poem gives her a window rather than a reflection — “She looked out the window and sighed” — and the speaker supplies what she may be seeing, without asking. The gap between generations is not bridged but honored. Both writers understand that the most important thing in a story is often the thing that is never said.
