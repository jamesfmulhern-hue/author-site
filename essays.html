Criticism & Craft

Essays

Short critical and personal essays on the recurring images, religious interpretation, governing methods, and working beliefs behind the fiction, poetry, and teaching of James Mulhern.

An Editor’s Note

On this series

These forty-two essays read James Mulhern’s work the way a careful reader would — following a single image, method, or conviction across stories, poems, and the classroom until it declares its own logic. They fall, roughly, into eight groups.

Recurring images and the governing method. Seven essays trace a single image or habit of mind across the whole body of work: fire, water, the sacred hidden inside shabby and ordinary detail, the sacrament of communion running through the classroom and the fiction and the poems, the repeated motifs (threshold, hand, water, the slant of light) read as a compositional system, Mulhern’s own habit of redrafting a single scene across a poem and a story and a second story, and a comprehensive synthesis of his craft signature, tropes, and allusive practice across the fiction, poetry, and teaching.

Single-work and full-length book studies. Five essays give a single book comprehensive, full-length treatment: the short story collection All We Ever Needed, traced through its themes, motifs, archetypes, allusions, and craft across all eight stories; a scholarly reading of “Assumptions” against the Catholic dogma of the Assumption of Mary; the Kirkus-starred novel Give Them Unquiet Dreams, read for its structure, its dual Boston and Achill Island geography, and its psychiatric plot; the novel Molly Bonamici, read for its Angel of Death premise, its Defoe inheritance, and the deadpan comedy the author has confirmed grew out of a single year of overwhelming loss; and A Prayer for Home, the earlier novella that first stages the storefront prophecy behind that premise and the widow whose real trip home turns out to be a grave.

The poetry, on its own terms. Four essays turn to Crossings specifically: its recurring imagery of water, touch, and inherited light; the second-person address that turns a reader into an accidental confessor for the dying and the dead; a structural reading of the collection’s twenty-poem sequence, from emigration to unresolved grief; and the objective correlatives — a folded robe, a photograph, a shattered devotional print — through which the poems hold grief without naming it.

Comparative and evaluative studies. Three essays place Mulhern beside other writers: a full-length evaluation within the Irish-American tradition alongside Farrell, Kennedy, McDermott, and Trevor; four shorter comparative readings beside James Joyce, Flannery O’Connor, and Raymond Carver; and a study of the allusive argument Molly Bonamici conducts with Fitzgerald’s Gatsby.

The author in his own words. Three essays draw directly on Mulhern’s own statements of belief: notes on attention and revision, a five-part statement of artistic creed, and an account of what he believes writing, teaching, and communion are for.

Narrative technique and craft mechanics. Six essays examine how the fiction is actually built: unreliable child narrators and the limits of innocence; the unlicensed confessors — grandmothers, children, a listener granted a decades-late absolution — who recur across the stories; the craft function of Aiden Glencar’s second sight as a solution to problems of foreshadowing and point of view; the plain, procedural diction of the reasonable criminal narrator in “Peppermint Candy” and “Mia Bambina”; the mechanics of dialogue itself — missing tags, action beats, the em dash — as a technical signature; and the deliberate etymology behind the names Molly Bonamici, Myra Bocca, and Peggy Fleming.

Recurring figures, traditions, and settings. Twelve essays place people, places, and genres under sustained examination: a single composite grandmother archetype traced by name and by deed across “A Bath,” “Assumptions,” and the Nonna cycle; the family elegy restraint of “A Bath” read against Alice Munro and Andre Dubus; the chosen family Molly Bonamici builds with her gay best friend in Wilton Manors, Florida; the coherent Irish-Catholic and Italian-American geography of Jamaica Plain, Braintree, and Revere; the Transcendentalist philosophy named directly in the author’s own Artistic Creed; the American confidence-woman tradition behind Lady Jane and Myra Bocca; a diptych on teaching as vocation and trap; the comic-grotesque lineage of Flannery O’Connor and J. M. Synge; Mr. Scarfone and the benign mafioso who makes a murder possible without ever becoming frightening; the precise sociolinguistic system by which profanity marks trust rather than breach; food as a sacramental economy of communion and poison; and Aiden Glencar’s second sight read directly against W. B. Yeats and The Celtic Twilight.

Editorial and bibliographic notes. Two essays turn to the history of the text itself: a descriptive bibliography of “Assumptions” across its four published texts, and a study of what psychological research on permeable mental boundaries can and cannot explain about a lifelong practice of recurrence.
