A Literary Essay · On Recurring Imagery

Unquenchable Fire

How a single scriptural phrase for damnation becomes, in James Mulhern’s fiction, the shimmer over a baptism.

“The waves beyond glimmered like sparks from an unquenchable fire.” — “Assumptions,” The Galway Review

Unquenchable fire is not neutral diction. In the Gospels it names a fire of judgment: Matthew 3:12 and Mark 9:43 give the phrase its threatening force. In “Assumptions,” the words are assigned to the shimmer of waves at the moment Peggy and Helen walk into the sea. The effect is not that judgment disappears. The story retains the phrase’s danger while setting it beside water, help, laughter, and a later rising. Its fire is therefore a contested image, not a decorative synonym for light.

The image belongs to a larger sequence. Peggy’s opening face is described through arid land, watercourses, and “red seas”; gorse burns yellow at the edge of the story’s Irish folk material; late-summer lightning flashes behind a gray curtain; and Titian’s Assunta is remembered in colors of “fire, flesh and blood.” These details do not prove a single hidden plot, but they prepare the reader to register the sea’s fire as part of an eschatological vocabulary rather than as beach scenery alone.

The sequence becomes more precise because the story occupies August 15. Its epigraph is Tota Pulchra Es, the Marian antiphon proper to the Feast of the Assumption, not merely a borrowed lyric of romantic praise. The story places that feast-day language beside a body marked by illness, damage, and social contempt. When Peggy, anointed with suntan oil, “shone like a miniature sun,” and when the final paragraph gives her the Sun’s great light, the changing scale of the image matters: a compromised body is seen under a light that neither ignores nor medically cures what has happened to it.

The appended Duffy poem offers a useful comparison. Duffy’s mother returns from the water “cheated of your surprise,” and his consolation is deferred. Mulhern uses similar language of being cheated of a surprise before Peggy reaches the sea, then sets the beach scene beside forceful water, collective laughter, help, and the sentence that she “rose from the sea.” The medical outcome remains unchanged: Peggy dies soon afterward. The story does not treat the day as empty.

That restraint matters to this reading. Fire in this story neither consumes every sinner nor guarantees a cure. It shifts familiar imagery of judgment into an invitation to look again. The failing body, the family’s casual cruelty, and the shabby setting remain fully visible. The glory, if the word is warranted, is not a substitute for them. It withholds a final verdict from contempt.

The light behaves the same way, and counting is what shows it. White appears five times in the story and does a different job each time: a white lie, a white light, a cloud that is not the soft fleecy white one expects, a white and gold sundress, and at last clothing “white as snow.” The sequence runs from a small domestic dishonesty to a transfiguring image, and it runs in that order. Nothing in the story asks the reader to forget the lie once the snow arrives. The imagery of glory is never handed over clean; it is assembled out of words that have already been used for weather, clothing, and untruth, which is why the final brightness reads as earned rather than imposed.
