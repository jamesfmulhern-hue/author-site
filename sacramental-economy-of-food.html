An Essay · On Food and Communion

The Sacramental Economy of Food

In the Fiction of James Mulhern — how cookies, candy, sugar, and whiskey carry love, obligation, and at last poison through the same trusted hands.

Abstract Mulhern’s own introduction to All We Ever Needed mentions food alongside water among the collection’s recurring concerns: the stories are populated by “a bath drawn for an aging parent, a ride to the beach, a call answered, a gift misunderstood” — ordinary gestures of care that the introduction says “do not erase suffering” but “reveal the stubborn human wish to be known and cared for despite everything.”1 The stories also invite a reading of food through a sacramental analogy: something prepared by one person is consumed by another and may carry care, obligation, or intention. This essay considers whether recurring food scenes do more than mark domestic tenderness, and how the collection’s two poisonings complicate that pattern.

I. A Eucharistic Logic Beneath the Kitchen Table

Mulhern’s own introduction to All We Ever Needed mentions food alongside water among the collection’s recurring concerns: the stories are populated by “a bath drawn for an aging parent, a ride to the beach, a call answered, a gift misunderstood” — ordinary gestures of care that the introduction says “do not erase suffering” but “reveal the stubborn human wish to be known and cared for despite everything.”1 The stories also invite a reading of food through a sacramental analogy: something prepared by one person is consumed by another and may carry care, obligation, or intention. This essay considers whether recurring food scenes do more than mark domestic tenderness, and how the collection’s two poisonings complicate that pattern.

II. Cookies as Grace: The Ordinary Sacrament

A basic form of the pattern is maternal and grand-maternal feeding, especially visible in “Mia Bambina.” When Nonna sends Molly away to college, she sends cookies with a note that combines nutrition, threat, and blessing: “Here are some cookies for the sweetest cookie in my life. You must swear to eat them all yourself!… A curse on you if you don’t eat every last one of these cookies yourself. I want you to be fatter the next time I see you… Nonna loves mia bambina.”2 The note’s swing from endearment to curse and back suggests that feeding a child is, for Nonna, an obligation with real stakes. The demand that Molly “swear” to eat them “yourself,” excluding the dormitory roommate, also marks the cookies as a private exchange rather than a general kindness.

III. The Same Vessel, Weaponized: “Sure, mia bambina. What’s one more ingredient?”

The horror of “Mia Bambina” is structural rather than merely plot-driven: Mulhern does not introduce a new, sinister object to carry out the murder. He reuses the exact vessel of tenderness already established — Nonna’s cookies — and simply adds an ingredient. When Molly needs to eliminate her roommate Ashley, she goes to the same grandmother, using the same register of family favor: “Getting the cyanide from Mr. Scarfone, a mobster friend of my grandmother’s, was easy. She was always saying she would do anything for her ’precious granddaughter,’ so when I asked her to bake the special cookies, she said, ’Sure, mia bambina. What’s one more ingredient? Not a problem.’”2 The line’s horror is entirely in its syntax: “what’s one more ingredient” treats cyanide as continuous with flour, sugar, and chocolate chips, a domestic addition rather than a moral rupture. Nonna’s willingness collapses the distance between nurture and murder into a single unbroken gesture of love — she is not persuaded to kill; she is asked to bake, and baking is what she has always done for this particular granddaughter. The novel’s closing image confirms the sacramental register explicitly: Molly plans to “compliment her on the effectiveness of her wording in the note,” noting that Nonna “would be happy to know that Ashley had died for her sins and that her cookies were a perfect hit”2 — the phrase “died for her sins,” borrowed intact from the vocabulary of atonement, recasts a poisoning as a kind of liturgical justice administered through food.

IV. Peppermint as Absolution and as Weapon

“Peppermint Candy” performs the identical operation in miniature and in reverse order — the poisoning precedes, rather than follows, the story’s use of candy as ordinary comfort. The narrator, a teacher retaliating against a student’s humiliation, prepares the weapon with clinical, almost priestly care: “I thought of the care I had taken to lightly rub Visine onto the candy before rewrapping it,”3 language that treats the rewrapping — restoring the candy’s innocent appearance — as part of the ritual’s completion, not an afterthought. The story’s final line performs the same collapse “Mia Bambina” performs: after poisoning one student, the narrator writes, “I retrieved the peppermints from my desk drawer and placed them in my satchel. I would share them again another day,”3 restoring the candy immediately to its ordinary, communal function of being shared. The peppermint does not stop being a treat because it has also been a poison; the story insists these are the same object serving the same social function — a small gift passed from an authority figure to a dependent — regardless of what has been added to it. This mirrors “Assumptions,” where peppermint candy first appears innocently, “enjoying the deliciousness of peppermint candy, swinging our legs together,” establishing the object’s baseline sweetness before the collection later corrupts it — the reader is trained, across the arc of the book, to receive peppermint as tenderness, which is precisely what makes its later weaponization effective rather than merely shocking.

V. Whiskey, Sugar Cubes, and the Communion of the Table

Not every food exchange in the corpus curdles into violence, and the essay’s argument depends on the fact that most do not: the sacramental logic is visible precisely because most transmissions remain benign, making the poisoned ones legible as corruptions of a norm rather than a separate category of event. In “Assumptions,” Peggy Fleming passes the young narrator a sugar cube “that she kept in her pocketbook in case her insulin dropped suddenly,” accompanying it with reassurance about a lost dog — “We saved Scruffy’s life, sweetheart… Sometimes that’s the way it has to be, my love”4 — a moment in which a diabetic woman’s emergency medical supply becomes, in the same gesture, a token of comfort passed to a grieving child. The child is in turn taught to “pour her whiskey sours,” his grandmother supervising the dosage (“Not too much, sweetheart”)4 — establishing a lineage of controlled substances (sugar, whiskey, later peppermint, later cyanide) moving hand to hand between generations, each transfer carrying both practical risk and emotional intention. The meal itself, not just its contents, repeatedly does theological work: the family’s habitual gathering around food and drink is explicitly a site of confession, testimony, and communal absolution, as when the whole extended family sits for “meatballs,” whiskey, and unresolved grievance in the “Myra Bocca” and Molly Bonamici material — food is the occasion under which secrets surface, not incidental to it.

VI. Conclusion: Feeding as the Family’s Native Grammar

Read across the collection, food can function as a recurring grammar — a set of gestures (prepare, offer, insist on consumption, receive) through which family members express what they do not say directly. Love, obligation, grief, and murder can all appear through feeding, an action that is direct and physical. The cyanide cookies and poisoned peppermints thus complicate a pattern readers have already seen associated with care. Mulhern’s introduction calls naming “a kind of devotion” and describes his stories as “prayers.”1 The food scenes suggest that a family’s care and its capacity for harm can emerge from the same kitchen and the same familiar hands.
