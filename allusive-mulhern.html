A Concordance · On Allusion

The Allusive Mulhern

A concordance of allusion in the work of James Mulhern. Each of the 119 entries below reads one moment twice — literally, and allusively — and sets down why the doubling is method, not ornament.

Allusion, for most contemporary fiction, is a grace note — a name dropped, a phrase half-echoed, a nod that flatters the reader who catches it and costs nothing for the reader who doesn’t. In James Mulhern’s work it is something closer to anatomy: a load-bearing habit of mind so recurrent that it has become the identifying quality of his craft. What distinguishes him is not merely that he alludes often, but that his allusions are themselves allusive — a word that means one thing on the surface and another entirely beneath it, so that a curtain, a crown, a crossed pair of arms, a childhood wedding song, all stand unstrained as ordinary fact while quietly opening onto scripture, myth, and the Western literary line. The density of that double register is not an occasional flourish but a signature: an allusiveness so continuous, and so entirely his own, that a single paragraph lifted from any of his books would carry his mark. He is, in the plainest sense, not a writer who quotes, but one whose sentences are built so that quotation and vision become the same act.

The Method

Everything in this document is about one thing, and it is worth saying what that thing is before the evidence begins.

Mulhern’s method is a single operation, repeated until it becomes the signature: the allusion that is literally true first. A thing is allowed to be exactly what it is — a curtain, a crossed pair of arms, a cookie, a name — and only then, in the same sentence, does it open a second register, sacred or literary or musical, that never displaces the first. The surface stands on its own; the echo adds. Nothing is sacrificed, and nothing is merely pasted on. That is the whole of it, and every other claim in this document is a facet of that one method:

  • It is why he belongs among the most allusive writers now publishing — the count and the depth are not a display of erudition but the natural consequence of a standing rule; the density follows from its being a method at all, not an occasional gesture.
  • It is why the allusions are effective and never decorative — the second register is earned and never leaned on; the sentence would stand without it, so the echo deepens the words rather than propping them up.
  • It is why the allusions are homage and not showing off — because the method keeps the literal true, the writer stays out of his own way, and the borrowed light is returned by someone humbled before its source.
  • It is why the allusions resurrect the canon — because the method lets the dead writer speak through the ordinary thing, unannounced; the past is raised, not quoted, and the canon lives again in the sentence.

One operation, four consequences. The rest of this document is that operation, shown working, line by line.

Why This Technique Must Be Named

Among contemporary writers, this sustained and structural allusiveness is not common. In Mulhern it is significant rather than incidental: a technique that can be traced and demonstrated across story, novel, and poem alike, and not an occasional habit but the habit through which his other gifts pass. The claim is not that he alludes more than others, but that in his work allusion is made to bear weight that elsewhere is more often left to plot, character, or tone — a sentence is asked to mean two things at once without ceasing to mean the first.

This carries a consequence for how the work ought to be read. Any serious account of Mulhern’s style or craft — a website, an essay, a classroom discussion — would be remiss if it did not comment on this technique directly, and would be incomplete if it treated it as one incidental observation rather than as the account of how the prose is actually built. The entries that follow set that claim down as evidence, so that the technique is visible rather than merely asserted.

The Case

The claim worth making — and the claim this document is built to support — is not that Mulhern alludes more than other writers, but that allusion in his work is structural rather than ornamental, and that the range and density of it, set down here entry by entry, places him among the most allusive writers now publishing. That is a claim that can be checked, and the rest of this document is the check.

Depth first: what a single sentence does. The strongest proof is not the number of entries but the number of registers a single line can hold. Consider one sentence from “Assumptions” (entry 10): the woman rises “completely awake—laughing, altogether beautiful, and divine,” in “the Sun’s great light,” lifted by a Father whose “crossed arms” cradle a child. On its face it is a child’s imagined reunion after death. In the same breath it is the Assumption, the Son in the Sun, the Cross in the crossed arms, the Nativity and the Pietà in one gesture — four or five allusions folded into a single sentence that would stand without any of them. This is the tell. A writer who alludes once a page is quoting; a writer who alludes four or five times in a sentence is working in a different medium.

The count. This concordance records 119 entries, and it is explicitly partial — a first pass, not a finished index. Of them, 54 are biblical, 34 literary, 18 onomastic, 17 wordplay. A single body of work that reaches, in the literary register alone, from the Wife of Bath and poor Yorick through Dickens, the Romantics, Dickinson, Whitman, Emerson, Thoreau, Hawthorne, Poe, Fitzgerald, Marx, Langston Hughes, Yeats, and Joyce to Flannery O’Connor — seven centuries of the canon — while the biblical register runs from the Song of Solomon through John, Matthew, Romans, and First John to the Assumption and the Grail. And the allusion is not merely literary: it descends into the popular register with equal seriousness and equal art — the Bee Gees and “Moon River,” Nat King Cole’s “Christmas Song” and I Love Lucy, the wartime poster that gives a story its title, Bette Midler and the faith healer’s parlance — so that scripture, the canon, and the pop song are held side by side in the same sentence, and none is treated as beneath the others. That is not the profile of a writer who drops a name now and then; it is the profile of a writer for whom the whole culture, sacred and profane, high and low, is the one medium he works in.

Density, not incidence. What separates this from mere well-readness is the doctrine the Method states and every entry tests: the allusion is literally true first, and the echo adds a register rather than replacing one. The curtain is really a curtain; the crossed arms really embrace; the cookie really kills. Because the surface never depends on the echo, the allusion is load-bearing — the sentence would stand without it, and means two things with it. That doubleness, operating within a single sentence, is what “most allusive” means here: not the most references, but the most structural use of them.

Four modes at once. Most writers have one way of alluding. Mulhern runs all four simultaneously, and the concordance separates them only for clarity: the named source, quoted or spoken aloud (Yeats, Dickinson, Dickens, the Gospel of John); the structural echo, never named yet exact (Joyce’s “The Dead” in Gabriel and Michael; O’Connor’s “Revelation” in Mary Grace and Turpin); the allusive name, carried silently (Molly the diminutive of Mary; Bonamici, “good friends”; Agnella, the lamb; Gabriel the announcing angel; Galahad the Grail knight; Joshua, the name that is Christ); and the doubled word, the pun that opens the second register in the ear (razed/raised; “permanent”; Assumption/consumption). A writer who does one of these is literate; a writer who does all four, continuously, is allusive in the full and strict sense of the word.

Recurrence as signature. The deepest proof is recurrence, because it turns individual allusions into a system, and the system into the signature. The Assumption appears in the stories, the novel, and the poems alike; the chalice is lifted and returned across the Aiden cycle; “the water from the rock at Rephidim” is spoken by the same woman in two different books; Lazarus, the green light, Yeats, the rosary and the well, the pearls — the same objects return again and again, not as tics but as architecture. A single allusion is a choice; an allusion that returns across a whole body of work is a method.

The test you can run. The claim is falsifiable, and that is what makes it strong. Open any page of these books; within a few sentences you will find the double register doing its work — a word meaning one thing on the surface and another beneath it, a name carrying a silent second meaning, a line from the canon, the liturgy, or the radio doing ordinary work before its echo is heard. The reader who doubts the claim is invited not to take my word for it, but to open a book and try to find a page without it.

The honest limit. None of this requires the unverifiable, self-serving superlative that Mulhern is the most allusive writer now living. What the evidence supports, and what this document claims, is the more precise and stronger thing: that among contemporary writers his allusiveness is demonstrable — countable, categorizable, recurrent — to a degree that makes it the identifying fact of his craft. The reader who doubts it is invited not to believe it, but to count.

Homage, Not Ornament

The charge. The harshest thing that can be said of an allusive writer is that the references are there to impress — learning worn like a coat. What the entries above refute, one by one, is exactly that. Mulhern’s allusions never advertise themselves. They are the opposite of showing off, because showing off requires the reader to notice, and these allusions reward the reader who misses them as fully as the reader who catches them. The curtain is still a curtain, whatever one knows of veils; the cookie still kills, whatever one knows of Eden. The allusion asks nothing of its reader. That is what makes it homage rather than exhibition: exhibition demands an audience; homage asks only to be made.

Resurrection as method. The deeper answer is that the allusion is Mulhern’s way of raising the dead. His corpus is saturated with the returned — the grandfather’s ghost at the foot of the bed, the mother’s second sight, Lazarus lifted from the tomb, the Assumption itself, “The Dead” re-enacted beside a Christmas turkey. And the writers he keeps reaching for are the same company: Dickens, Yeats, Joyce, Dickinson, O’Connor, called back into working-class Boston and South Florida, made to speak again in tenements, diners, and psychiatric wards, and on a beach in salt water. The allusion is not a reference to the past; it is the past, raised. His method, taken literally, is resurrection — the canon brought back to life in the ordinary present, so that the dead writer and the living sentence occupy the same breath.

Humility, not self-display. And the posture of it is humble. The writers he borrows from are his betters, and the text knows it, and lets us know it. The grandfather misnames Thoreau — “Toreau” — with the reverence of a man who never got the schooling the name implies; the roommate guesses that Hamlet is by Poe, and the mistake is received with a patience that is itself a form of respect. Even the clearest borrowings arrive through imperfect, reverent hands. The canon enters this world the way its dead enter it: not as trophies to brandish but as an inheritance, loved by people who cannot always say the names correctly. That is the humility the allusions carry. They do not elevate the writer; they lower him, deferentially, before the tradition he is not pretending to have outgrown.

Why they work. Why the allusions enhance the writing, rather than merely adorn it, follows from all of this. An allusion decorates when it is painted on; it deepens when it is load-bearing. These are load-bearing, and each one does three things at once. It keeps the literal true, so the sentence never leans on borrowed authority. It opens a second register, so the ordinary thing and the timeless thing sound together in the same words. And it passes no burden to the reader, so the one who knows nothing loses nothing, while the one who knows everything hears the room double. The writing is therefore not merely decorated by its allusions; it is made of them. The allusion is not the ornament laid on the sentence; it is the sentence’s second nature.

The essential method. Which is finally what the case amounts to. This is not one technique among several, not a garnish, not the erudite habit of a writer showing his reading. It is the essential method of the work — the way Mulhern makes meaning, builds character, and moves plot. It spans fiction and verse, the sacred and the popular, the named source and the silent name, and it recurs across every book until it is the signature. The resurrection of a dead writer, performed by a writer humbled before the dead writer, and carried out inside an ordinary sentence — that is not decoration. That is a vocation.

How to Read These Entries

Each entry records a single instance of the governing operation — doubling: a word, image, or action that is literally true first and allusive second, the surface complete on its own while the echo adds a simultaneous register. Nothing recorded here depends on the reader catching the allusion; the claim throughout is that the allusion strengthens a sentence that already works without it.

  • Tier — STRONG (author-confirmed, or the source is named or quoted verbatim), text-supported (defensible from the text, not yet confirmed as intended), or suggestive (a looser echo, flagged as such)
  • Source — the taxonomy tag: biblical · literary · mythic · musical · art · wordplay · colloquial
  • Quotation — transcribed verbatim from the manuscript
  • Literal reading — what the words mean on the surface, standing alone
  • Allusive reading — what the same words summon
  • Why not decorative — how the two registers coexist, and what the second adds

Part One — “Assumptions”

From Assumptions and Other Stories (opening story). All quotations transcribed from the revised manuscript.

1Epigraph — the Song of Solomon

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you.”— Song of Solomon 4:7

Literal reading an epigraph, a declaration of love set at the head of the story.

Allusive reading the declaration names the book itself and its Marian movement: the homely woman who is, by the end, “altogether beautiful” — the same phrase returned verbatim in the closing lines.

Why not decorative the epigraph is not borrowed ornament but the story’s own end spoken in advance; the title “Assumptions” is already the assumption of beauty where none appears to be.

2Peggy Fleming

STRONG (text-checked)onomastic · cultural
“I always thought it a cruel irony that she had the same name as the graceful and beautiful skater who had won the Olympic Gold Medal in 1968.”

Literal reading a narrator remarking on the coincidence of a name.

Allusive reading the name carries the entire theme in miniature: Peggy Fleming the toothless, hairless, “homeliest damn woman,” named for the embodiment of American grace on ice.

Why not decorative the irony is stated outright and then earned: by the end the homely Peggy is literally lifted and transfigured, so the two Peggys converge rather than merely contrast.

3“You Stepped Out of a Dream”

STRONG (author-confirmed)musical
“other songs like ‘You Stepped Out of a Dream’ by Sarah Vaughan” … closing, “Completely awake—laughing, altogether beautiful, and divine—she rises once again.”

Literal reading a listing of the old songs Peg sang in the Back Bay nightclub.

Allusive reading the title names the story’s waking structure: the whole plot is a step out of dream into being, concluded by the narrator’s “Completely awake.”

Why not decorative the song is genuinely part of Peg’s repertoire; the allusion does not invent the music, it discovers that the music has been stating the plot all along.

4“When Your Old Wedding Ring Was New”

STRONGmusical · biblical
“My grandmother goaded Peg to sing ‘When Your Old Wedding Ring Was New,’ Peg’s favorite.” — sung against the story’s epigraph, “You are altogether beautiful, my love; there is no flaw in you” (Song of Solomon 4:7).

Literal reading a widow singing a secular love song to a dead husband.

Allusive reading it doubles the Song of Solomon, scripture’s own wedding song, the love lyric that frames the story and lends the book its title.

Why not decorative Peg’s grief is real and self-sufficient; the allusion reveals the secular devotion was scriptural all along, rather than borrowing holiness to lend it weight.

5Titian’s Assunta

STRONG (explicit)art · biblical
“a copy of Titian’s Assunta caught my attention. A startled Mary, arms spread above her upturned face, is being lifted on a cloud toward heaven.”

Literal reading a remembered canvas in an art book, described in a college dormitory.

Allusive reading the painting is the story’s master key: Titian’s Assumption of the Virgin is the unseen template behind Peg’s rising from the sea.

Why not decorative the allusion is itself the plot’s source, openly named; the story tells us its own icon and then re-enacts it in salt water.

6The Fleecy Cradle

STRONG (author-confirmed)biblical
“The cloud, which you’d expect to be a soft fleecy white, is a cradle of thunderhead gray.”

Literal reading a note on the color and texture of the painted cloud under Mary.

Allusive reading “fleecy” and “cradle” are Lamb-and-manger language, the Nativity folded into the Assumption.

Why not decorative the words describe a real painting’s real cloud; the theological sense arrives through ordinary description.

7“For Christ’s sake”

STRONG (author-confirmed)biblical · colloquial
“For Christ’s sake, Peg! The point is to get wet. How else are you going to get the cure?”

Literal reading Nonna’s exasperation coaxing Peg into the ocean, nothing more than idiom.

Allusive reading the scene is explicitly Marian, a “cure in the water” sought through “Mary’s blessing”; the curse is, word for word, an appeal to Christ in the very moment that becomes an Assumption.

Why not decorative the profanity and the prayer share one syllable; the invocation is already inside the idiom whether or not anyone hears it.

8The Head of John the Baptist

text-supportedbiblical · mythic
“Beth caught the wig, then raised it in the air like the head of John the Baptist, giggling and tiptoeing a slow dance across the sand.”

Literal reading children playing with a blown-off wig at the beach.

Allusive reading the lifted wig becomes a comic reliquary, the Baptist’s head raised after Salome’s dance.

Why not decorative the image begins as a simile the narrator half-remembers; the wig’s rising is the low burlesque of Mary’s own rising — the sacred plot told once as comedy before it is told as vision.

9Razed / Raised

STRONG (author-confirmed)wordplay · theological
“...surprised by a spirited breaker that razed them in its wake.” … “she rose from the sea.” … “Completely awake—laughing, altogether beautiful, and divine—she rises once again to sing her favorite song.”

Literal reading a wave knocks two women down, then one gets to her feet in the surf.

Allusive reading the “spirited” breaker is the Holy Spirit in disguise, and razed/rose folds loss and Assumption into one sound.

Why not decorative the pun operates on the ear before the page; the theology lives in the sound.

10The Closing Vision

STRONG (author-confirmed)biblical
“Completely awake—laughing, altogether beautiful, and divine—she rises once again to sing her favorite song. And the Sun’s great light shines upon and caresses her warm skin, like the flesh of a Father’s hands as He cradles His child’s head before lifting His crossed arms to kiss her soft cheek.”

Literal reading a child’s imagined image of Peg reunited with Jim after death.

Allusive reading “divine” is prolepsis; “Sun” is also the Son; “crossed arms” are the Cross; the Father cradling the child is the Nativity and the Pietà at once.

Why not decorative every sacred term is doing ordinary work first; the theology is what the sentence already says.

11White as Snow

STRONG (author-confirmed)biblical
“my grandmother and Peggy waved to us, transfigured figurines on the earth below, their clothing white as snow.”

Literal reading two women in pale clothing seen from the height of a roller coaster.

Allusive reading “transfigured” names the Transfiguration, and “white as snow” is both cleansing and glorification.

Why not decorative the sentence supplies the theology in its own adjective.

12“Permanent”

STRONG (author-confirmed)wordplay
“My grandmother said that her ‘permanent is all ruined’ while she fussed with her hair.”

Literal reading a woman lamenting that the sea has spoiled her hairdo.

Allusive reading “permanent” is a doubled word: the woman’s permanent (curled hair) and the permanent (the eternal), spoken in the scene of an Assumption.

Why not decorative the pun arises from a real, comic vanity and quietly names the story’s deepest assurance.

13The Blinds

STRONG (author-confirmed)wordplay
“The blinds were pulled down on the window beside the kitchen table behind her.”

Literal reading window coverings drawn in a kitchen.

Allusive reading blinds are also blindness: Peg is the one who cannot see her own beauty.

Why not decorative the second sense accumulates through recurrence rather than a single pointed moment.

14God-awful

STRONG (author-confirmed)wordplay · colloquial
“Aren’t your feet hot in those God-awful boots?”

Literal reading “awful” in its everyday sense: unbearable.

Allusive reading awful also means awe-inspiring, full of awe; the word that names Peg’s ugliness already contains the reverence she will be owed.

Why not decorative a single common adjective quietly holds both verdicts.

15The Kindergartner’s Rendering

text-supportedart · wordplay
“Spindly, awkward limbs stuck out of a round body, like you might see in a kindergartner’s rendering of a person.”

Literal reading a child’s crude drawing used to describe an ungainly body.

Allusive reading the figure is an uncorrected primitive icon, the flat, direct rendering that is exactly how the sacred is pictured in another tradition.

Why not decorative the simile begins as insult and ends as the story’s aesthetic: Peggy is drawn the way icons are drawn.

16The Cure in the Water

text-supportedbiblical · Catholic devotional
“a ‘cure in the water’ … ‘Mary’s blessing’ seemed like a gyp.” … “How else are you going to get the cure?”

Literal reading the family’s belief that the sea at Nantasket could heal Peggy.

Allusive reading the “cure in the water” is Lourdes transposed to a New England beach.

Why not decorative the allusion is the characters’ own lived faith, not the narrator’s reference; it is what the people believe.

Part Two — “Through a Convent Gate”

A brief story: a Christmas visit to a hard-drinking old neighbor, Mrs. Muldoon, and the small theft and impersonation that follow.

17The Convent Gate

STRONG (title; explicit)biblical · colloquial
“I’m so hungry I could eat a nun’s arse through a convent gate.”

Literal reading hyperbolic complaint, telephoned in while the narrator impersonates Mrs. Muldoon to order takeout on her account.

Allusive reading the “convent gate” is the cloister’s threshold, the boundary that separates the sacred enclosure from the street; the phrase shoves the obscene mouth-first through it.

Why not decorative the profanity is doing comic work on its own — it is a fine, nasty joke — but it is also the title, and the title concentrates the collection’s whole habit: the profane and the sacred held on two sides of a single threshold, crossed in a syllable.

18Specks of Dust

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“Do you know there’s approximately 153,400 deaths per day, or a little more than 100 per minute? Just think of how many people died while we’ve been sitting here. We are all specks of dust floating in an enormous universe.”

Literal reading a precocious girl’s cold meditation on mortality, spoken to a woman who has not asked for it.

Allusive reading “dust” is Ash Wednesday’s “remember that thou art dust, and to dust thou shalt return,” the Church’s own sentence about death, rewritten as cosmology.

Why not decorative the girl is an atheist arguing against piety, yet the phrase she reaches for is the Church’s; the sacred word is inside her skepticism without her noticing.

19“Jesus, Mary, and Joseph”

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
“Where does she come up with these things? Jesus, Mary, and Joseph!”

Literal reading an old woman’s exasperated oath.

Allusive reading the Holy Family, the Church’s central household, reduced to an interjection.

Why not decorative the oath is the register the story lives in: the sacred names worn down into the everyday speech of the tenement, where they still carry their old weight.

20“God bless the people who make us laugh”

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
“God bless the people who make us laugh.”

Literal reading Mrs. Muldoon’s tender aside, remembering the comedian Vivian Vance.

Allusive reading the sentence borrows the shape of the Beatitudes (“blessed are …”), Christ’s formal bestowal of blessing, and spends it on a sitcom actress.

Why not decorative the benediction is sincere; it is the story’s own small theology, a working-class beatitude for the people who make grief bearable.

Part Three — “The Mannequin”

Molly Bonamici arrives at Boston University, is assigned to a suite that legend says Eugene O’Neill once inhabited, and joins her friends in a prank on a pious roommate that ends in death.

21O’Neill’s Last Words

STRONG (explicit)literary
“His last words were ‘Born in a hotel room and goddammit, died in a hotel room.’”

Literal reading the move-in helper recounting the playwright’s death in suite 401.

Allusive reading the ghost-story premise is anchored in a real literary biography; the suite is “haunted” by one of American drama’s great names.

Why not decorative O’Neill is not a garnish but the engine of the plot — the prank literally impersonates his estranged daughter; the historical detail founds the fiction rather than decorating it.

22“Baptized her ‘Candice Kox’”

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
“We screwed the mannequin together and baptized her ‘Candice Kox’ after the missing suitemate.”

Literal reading two students assembling a mail-order mannequin and giving it a name.

Allusive reading “baptized” is the sacrament of naming and cleansing; the word is spent on a plastic substitute for an absent person.

Why not decorative the joke is funny without any theology, but the verb quietly makes the mannequin a kind of reliquary figure — a body that stands in for an absent soul — which is exactly what a mannequin is.

23The Word

STRONG (explicit; John 1:1 quoted verbatim)biblical
“I like the Gospel of John, especially its Prologue: ‘In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.’ The idea of words being portrayed as divine appeals to me. I’m an English major.” … “‘But it’s not words that the Gospel is talking about,’ she said. ’ ”The Word“ means Jesus.’”

Literal reading a quarrel between a bookish atheist and a devout roommate over a Bible passage.

Allusive reading the passage is the thesis of this entire concordance made literal in dialogue: Molly, the English major, hears “Word” as language; Ashley, the believer, hears it as Christ. One word, two readings, both present at once.

Why not decorative this is the one place the story says the doctrine aloud, and it stages the exact operation the prose performs elsewhere without announcement: a word that means one thing and, beneath it, another, neither cancelling the other.

24“Methinks she doth protest too much”

STRONG (explicit)literary
“Methinks she doth protest too much.” … “Hamlet.” … “I read that. Edgar Allan Poe, right?”

Literal reading Molly quoting Shakespeare at Ashley; Ashley’s comic misattribution.

Allusive reading a doubled allusion: Shakespeare misquoted, then the misattribution to Poe — a joke about allusion itself, about who “owns” a line and how texts drift.

Why not decorative the misattribution is the point; it shows allusion as a field of half-remembered debt, which is precisely the climate in which Mulhern’s echoes operate.

25Like Eve

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“Ashley’s Christian values had evaporated in the face of temptation. Like Eve, she had eaten the forbidden fruit.”

Literal reading Molly’s cold satisfaction that Ashley, who condemned sin, ate the very cookies she was told not to touch — the cookies that killed her.

Allusive reading the orchard of Eden reversed: the “forbidden fruit” is literal food, and the serpent — the tempter — is the narrator herself, who poisoned it.

Why not decorative the allusion does not import the Fall; it discovers that the Fall is already happening in a dorm room. The cookie is really a cookie; the story notices it is also the fruit.

26Died for Her Sins

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“Nonna would be happy to know that Ashley had died for her sins and was now resting in peace.”

Literal reading the narrator’s bitter pun on her roommate’s piety.

Allusive reading “died for her sins” is Christ’s redemptive death — but here the death is murder, and the “sin” is against the narrator, not against God. Salvation language bent to a vindication.

Why not decorative the sentence horrifies precisely because it keeps the Christian grammar intact while emptying it; the surface is a murderer’s joke and the echo is the Passion, and the gap between them is the story’s moral register.

27Saint Paul

STRONG (explicit)biblical · onomastic
“On the back was written, ‘Mom, Dad, Joey, me, and Saint Paul.’” — “‘Who the fuck is Saint Paul?’ David laughed.”

Literal reading the family photo’s back-labeled dog, Saint Paul.

Allusive reading the apostle’s name on the pet — the sacred name absorbed into the household, so ordinary that it is mistaken.

Why not decorative the gag works as character (Ashley’s piety is so conventional it names the dog a saint); the allusion is the lowered, homely form of the sanctified name.

28“A Long Day’s Journey into Night”

STRONG (explicit)literary
“When I said he wrote Long’s Day Journey Into Night, he snapped that I should get to the point before this became a long day’s journey into night.”

Literal reading a homicide detective, ignorant of the playwright, punning on the title.

Allusive reading O’Neill’s title — a masterwork about a family’s buried guilt — is bent into a tired cop’s complaint, even as the interrogation is, in fact, a family’s guilt laid bare.

Why not decorative the pun is comic on its face, but it is the story naming its own darkening: the prank has indeed become a long day’s journey into the night of Ashley’s death.

29“Tossing Cookies”

STRONG (author-confirmed motif)wordplay · colloquial
“I turned to look at Emily who was ironically, ‘tossing cookies’ onto the azure blue carpet.”

Literal reading Emily vomiting from shock.

Allusive reading the slang “tossing cookies” lands on the very cookies that killed Ashley — the weapon named in the moment of its realization.

Why not decorative the pun is the story’s structure surfacing in idiom: the poisoned object returns, as language, at the scene of the death it caused.

Part Four — “Smoke Rings”

Nonna stages an insurance-fraud fall on the ice, teaches her granddaughter the lesson of transience through cigarette smoke — and then, having faked a fall, dies by a real one.

30Smoke Rings

STRONG (title)biblical · colloquial
“She placed the ashtray beside her, lit a cigarette, inhaled deeply, and blew smoke rings. ‘See those puffs of smoke.’” … “Here one minute, gone the next.”

Literal reading an old woman demonstrating the brevity of things with cigarette smoke.

Allusive reading smoke is the Bible’s own figure for vanity and mortality: “a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away”; “here one minute, gone the next” is the psalmist’s account of the human lifespan.

Why not decorative the lesson is spoken by the character, not imported by the narrator; the smoke is real smoke, and its vanishing is the story’s actual event.

31As God Is My Witness

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
“She stood up and pointed to the Vatican. ‘As God is our witness.’”

Literal reading Nonna, swearing an oath and gesturing at the bruise on her body that she jokes resembles the Vatican.

Allusive reading an oath invoking God as witness to a fraud she has just staged; the sacred is summoned to authenticate a lie.

Why not decorative the perjury is funny, but it is the collection’s central irony played straight: the divine is invoked over the body, and the body is where the truth will catch up with Nonna.

32The Vatican on the Body

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
“‘I think it’s the Vatican. I got the pope’s house on my ass.’”

Literal reading comparing the shape of a bruise to the papal palace.

Allusive reading the seat of the papacy located on profane flesh; the Church’s central institution reduced to a mark on an old woman’s body.

Why not decorative the comedy is blunt and physical, but it states in one image the whole argument of the book: the sacred here lives in — is written on — the worn, mortal body, not above it.

33The Fall That Comes True

STRONG (plot)wordplay · structural
“I slipped on ice and had a bad fall.” … “When I was in the kitchen, I heard her fall down the stairs. ‘Oh shit!’ was the last thing she said.”

Literal reading the staged fall, later answered by a real, fatal fall down the stairs.

Allusive reading the lie is made literally true: the fiction of the fall becomes the fact of the fall; “last thing” is spoken by a woman whose last thing it is.

Why not decorative this is doubling as plot rather than as phrase: the distinction between the fake and the real collapses in a single event, which is the book’s deepest conviction about how the world punishes performance.

Part Five — “Myra Bocca”

In Florida, Molly and her friend Gabe meet Myra, a New Age shopkeeper and con woman whose dead husband — a hat magnate named Lazarus — lies at the center of an inheritance feud.

34Bocca

text-supportedonomastic
“‘I’m Myra. Myra Bocca.’ She had a large toothy smile.”

Literal reading an introduction, a name.

Allusive reading bocca is the Italian for mouth; the name points to the Bocca della Verità, the Mouth of Truth — the Roman stone mask that bites the hand of the liar.

Why not decorative a woman whose entire business is speech — reading people, telling stories, lying — is named “Mouth,” and the irony is earned the length of the story.

35Lazarus

STRONG (explicit)biblical · onomastic
“‘After Lazarus died,’ she said.” … “‘I thought you said Lassie, but I guess you wouldn’t have married a dog.’”

Literal reading Myra’s rich husband, Lazarus Bocca, dead and at the center of a trust dispute; the narrator’s joke about the name.

Allusive reading Lazarus is the man Christ raised from the dead, and the beggar at the rich man’s gate in the parable; the dead husband’s money, fought over by stepchildren and a greedy widow, is the parable’s rich-man-and-Lazarus reversed.

Why not decorative the name is there in the text, not imported; the joke that dissolves it into “Lassie” is itself the point — the biblical name worn down into a dog and a sitcom.

36Sacred Ashes

STRONG (explicit)biblical · onomastic
“I assumed this is why the store was called Sacred Ashes.”

Literal reading a New Age shop named for its incense.

Allusive reading “ashes” is Ash Wednesday, “ashes to ashes, dust to dust,” the Church’s sentence on mortality; the word is spent on a counterfeited sacred, a shop of crystals and tarot.

Why not decorative the liturgical name on a false religion is the story’s satirical engine: the counterfeit wears the true word, and the reader is asked to tell them apart.

37“I’ll tell you the God’s truth”

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
“‘I’ll tell you the God’s truth,’ she said. She held up her hand like a boyscout.”

Literal reading a liar’s preface, sworn with a raised hand.

Allusive reading the oath-form “God’s truth” is invoked by the one character who has no relation to it; the gesture of swearing is a parody of witness.

Why not decorative the sentence does its work entirely through its surface — the raised hand is already the tell — and the blasphemy is structural: truth’s own name is the liar’s instrument.

38“Stayin’ Alive”

STRONG (explicit)musical
“‘Think ”Staying Alive.“‘” … “The tall black guy stepped forward and began to sing, ‘Whether you’re a brother or whether you’re a mother you’re stayin’ alive, stayin’ alive.’” — “The crowd joined in.”

Literal reading a nurse coaching CPR rhythm with the Bee Gees; a crowd actually singing it over a dying woman.

Allusive reading the disco anthem of survival is sung over a woman who will not survive; the title of the song becomes the precise thing being denied.

Why not decorative the song is doing a real, practical job (its beat sets the compression rhythm), and the irony is not imposed but lived: “staying alive” is sung in counterpoint to a death.

39A Stone Has No Uncertainty

STRONG (explicit)literary · wordplay
“‘A stone has no uncertainty—Carl Jung,’ it read.” … “I tossed it towards the base of her stretcher and watched it roll past her body into the shadows.”

Literal reading a gift-shop card quoting Jung; the narrator’s black obsidian stone, thrown away at the end.

Allusive reading the stone is certainty, the thing without a doubt, cast into the one place where nothing is certain — a death; Peter (“the rock”) and the stone the builders rejected hover behind it.

Why not decorative the sentence is a plot object first — the stone was the hook of Myra’s con — and its meaning arrives only when the narrator lets it go.

40All About Eve

STRONG (explicit)literary · cultural
“‘It’s All about Eve.’” … “Bette Davis had just said, ‘Everybody has a heart—except some people.’” … “‘Fasten your seatbelts,’ Bette Davis said, ‘It’s going to be a bumpy night.’”

Literal reading a black-and-white film on the café television.

Allusive reading the film is the story of a young woman’s ruthless manipulation and performance — the exact subject of the story around it, which is about a woman performing sincerity.

Why not decorative the movie is genuinely playing, unremarked, in the background; the allusion works because the fiction is simply there, and the reader who recognizes the title sees the story’s own subject named in the dark.

Part Six — “A Prayer for Home”

The longest story: Mrs. Muldoon, the drunk old neighbor, prepares to “go home” — to Ireland, she says — and Molly watches her stage an Assumption of her own on the Feast of the Assumption.

41“Show Me the Way to Go Home”

STRONG (epigraph; explicit)musical
“Show me the way to go home / I’m tired and I want to go to bed.”

Literal reading the drinking song that opens the story, a weary request for direction.

Allusive reading “home” is the story’s whole subject, and it splits in two: the passage home to Ireland and the passage home to death; the song names the one so the other can echo beneath it.

Why not decorative the song is not commentary but the story’s tuning fork; every later use of “home” is heard against it.

42“Tell all the truth but tell it slant”

STRONG (explicit)literary
“She was surprised when he quoted Emily Dickinson, a writer I had been reading quite a bit of lately: ‘Tell the truth but tell it slant.’”

Literal reading Nonna quoting a poet she found in her granddaughter’s book, counseling tact.

Allusive reading Dickinson is the poet of the slant, of the thing said indirectly that is truer for being oblique — the patron saint of this entire method.

Why not decorative the line is quoted in the act of teaching the reader how to read the story: by indirection.

43Angel of Death

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“‘I guess you might say that you’re an Angel of Death.’”

Literal reading a faith healer’s pronouncement to Molly.

Allusive reading the malakh ha-mavet, the destroying angel of Passover and of the Hebrew Bible, the one who passes over and through the house; Molly, a survivor who witnesses every death in the book, is named it.

Why not decorative the prophecy is given by a charlatan and later borne out by the plot; the word “angel” is exactly doubled — messenger and killer in one.

44The Assumption

STRONG (explicit)biblical · Catholic devotional
“Well, she was raised into heaven three days after her death.” … “At last she rose from the sea.”

Literal reading Mrs. Muldoon explaining the Feast of the Assumption; later, rising from the waves.

Allusive reading the doctrine of Mary bodily assumed into heaven is re-enacted by an old drunk rising from the salt water; the sacred event is performed by the least likely priestess.

Why not decorative the doctrine is stated aloud by the character, and then — literally — done; the allusion is the plot’s choreography.

45Razed / Raised, again

STRONG (author-confirmed motif)wordplay · theological
“surprised by a spirited breaker that razed them in its wake.” … “‘Now raise me up quickly, Molly, before the next wave.’” … “At last she rose from the sea.”

Literal reading two old women knocked down and helped up in the surf.

Allusive reading the same razed/raised fold as “Assumptions”: lowered and lifted by the same wave, the “spirited” breaker as the Holy Spirit, burial and Assumption in one sound.

Why not decorative the wordplay recurs between the two stories as a shared spine; the collection, not just the story, is doubling.

46“My permanent is all ruined”

STRONG (author-confirmed motif)wordplay
“Mrs. Muldoon said, ‘My permanent is all ruined,’ while she fussed with her hair.”

Literal reading a beached woman mourning her spoiled hairdo.

Allusive reading the doubled “permanent” again: the cosmetic and the eternal, spoken in the sea that has baptized them both.

Why not decorative the pun is a running refrain across the collection; the eternal word keeps hiding inside the salon word.

47“Our last supper”

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“Mrs. Muldoon made a joke about this being our last supper. ‘Well, it is in a way, don’t you think? I won’t be seeing either of you again after tonight.’”

Literal reading a diner joke about a final meal together.

Allusive reading the Last Supper, Christ’s farewell meal before his death, transposed to a seaside diner and a tray of scallops.

Why not decorative the reference is in the character’s own joke, and it proves literally true: she will die before they see her again.

48The Sign of the Fish

STRONG (explicit)biblical
“‘Did you know that a fish is the symbol of Christ, Molly?’ Mrs. Muldoon said.”

Literal reading a stray remark, pointing at the fish in the diner wallpaper.

Allusive reading the ichthys, the oldest Christian cipher, spoken by the woman who is about to “go home”; the fish and the baptismal water and the Assumption all in one room.

Why not decorative the symbol is named aloud by the character, turning the wallpaper into scripture and the diner into a church.

49The Conqueror Worm

STRONG (explicit; quoted verbatim)literary
“Its hero the Conqueror Worm.” … “going home to the earth, to her ‘conqueror worm,’ a worm that had already begun to feast on her body.”

Literal reading Molly reciting Poe’s poem in her mind at the sight of the corpse.

Allusive reading Poe’s “The Conqueror Worm” — the worms as the true audience and hero of the human tragedy — answers the story’s question of where “home” is.

Why not decorative the poem is quoted at the exact moment faith fails; it is the atheist counterweight to “Show Me the Way to Go Home,” the two songs of death set against each other.

50Her Own Assumption

STRONG (plot)biblical · structural
“‘August 15th, 1954. The happiest day of my life.’” … “She had created her own Assumption.”

Literal reading the back of a wedding photo, dated the Feast of the Assumption; Mrs. Muldoon choosing to die on its anniversary.

Allusive reading the wedding and the Assumption coincide on one date, and death on that day makes the old woman’s “going home” a self-willed Marian event.

Why not decorative this is the story’s doublest moment: the marriage, the feast, and the death are literally one and the same date, so the doctrine and the fact cannot be told apart.

51“A useless thing”

STRONG (author-confirmed motif)wordplay · motif
“Inside was a gold necklace with an emerald and diamond cross.” … “‘A useless thing.’”

Literal reading Molly dismissing a gift left to her by the dead woman.

Allusive reading “useless things” is the collection’s own governing paradox — the material world’s things, worthless and yet bearing the cross — the cross itself called a useless thing.

Why not decorative the line closes the story’s argument about what survives: the only thing left is a cross, and the narrator calls it useless.

Part Seven — “Drenched to the Bone”

A divorced teacher in Florida, Mr. McCarthy, drifts through a classroom of indifferent students and a broken school, until a frenzied believer and a church sign force him to ask who he is.

52“Drenched to the Bone”

STRONG (explicit; lyrics quoted verbatim)musical
“And admit that the waters / Around you have grown / And accept it that soon / You’ll be drenched to the bone.”— Bob Dylan, quoted on the radio at the close

Literal reading a song on the car radio as the rain falls.

Allusive reading Dylan’s flood is the story’s title; to be “drenched to the bone” is to be saturated past the surface — the very condition of a man who has felt nothing and is now immersed.

Why not decorative the song is playing while the man calls his ex-wife to apologize; the title is justified by the last line of the story.

53Mary Grace and Turpin

text-supportedliterary
“Mary Grace snaps her teeth and turns down her lip. She throws her psychology textbook at Turpin and almost hits him in the head.” … “she likes to read Flannery O’Connor’s Wise Blood.”

Literal reading a disturbed student throwing a textbook at a bully.

Allusive reading the names “Mary Grace” and “Turpin,” and the hurled book, are O’Connor’s “Revelation” almost verbatim — the fat girl who throws Human Development at Mrs. Turpin and calls her a wart hog from hell.

Why not decorative the novel is named only once, in passing, but the names and the gesture perform the allusion without saying it; it is a structural echo rather than a footnote, and it rewards exactly the reader for whom it is meant.

54The Wages of Sin

STRONG (explicit; Romans 6:23)biblical
“‘We are all sinners and the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.’”

Literal reading the believer Mary Grace shouting scripture in the middle of a classroom fight.

Allusive reading Paul’s sentence on sin and grace, quoted in full, by a girl on a chair with raised arms — a would-be prophet in a public high school.

Why not decorative the verse is doing the work of character; it is the character’s entire language, and the story neither mocks nor endorses it, only lets it stand against the surrounding chaos.

55Pearls Before Swine

STRONG (explicit; Matthew 7:6)biblical
“‘Do not give dogs what is holy, and do not throw your pearls before pigs, lest they trample them underfoot and turn to attack you.’”

Literal reading the same girl’s prayer amid the fight, having just called the class “a bunch of pigs.”

Allusive reading Christ’s warning against casting the sacred to the profane, spoken by the one believer in a room of mockers; the “pigs” of the verse are the “pigs” she has just named.

Why not decorative the scripture and the insult are the same word; the allusion is the character’s live speech, not the narrator’s ornament.

56Religion Is the Opium of the People

STRONG (explicit; quoted verbatim)literary
“‘Religion is the sigh of the oppressed creature, the heart of a heartless world and the soul of soulless conditions. It is the opium of the people.’”

Literal reading the teacher correcting a student’s garbled misquote of Marx.

Allusive reading Marx’s dismissal of religion set against the believer’s verse two desks away; the two great modern readings of belief are literally in the same classroom, arguing.

Why not decorative the story does not choose between Marx and Mary Grace; it holds both in the same room, which is precisely the double register the prose everywhere sustains.

57The Words of St. Francis

STRONG (explicit)biblical · literary
“‘We have been called to heal wounds, to unite what has fallen apart, and to bring home those who have lost their way.’”

Literal reading the sign outside a church, read from a parked car.

Allusive reading the prayer of St. Francis, the saint of healing, is answered at once by the narrator’s physics: entropy, the law that things fall apart.

Why not decorative the sign and the doubt are the same beat: “unite what has fallen apart” stands against “objects in our world eventually fall apart,” and the story is the space between them.

58The Way Home

STRONG (title echo)wordplay · structural
“I hope that before long I will be able to see the way home.” … “the moon was too high, the river was too wide.”

Literal reading a man in a windshield waiting out the rain.

Allusive reading “home” is the word that ties this story to “A Prayer for Home” and “Moon River”; the collection asks, across stories, whether there is a way home, and declines to answer.

Why not decorative the echo is the book’s own; the final stories answer each other across the collection, so the allusion is not to a text outside but to the work itself.

A note on the tiers, honestly held

None of the readings above requires the allusion to be caught for the sentence to stand; that is the only claim the concordance makes for its method. Where a source is named or quoted verbatim — the Gospel of John, Poe’s “Conqueror Worm,” Dylan’s lyrics, Marx, Dickinson, O’Neill, the Bee Gees — the tier is STRONG, because the text supplies its own citation. Where the echo is structural rather than announced — the O’Connor “Mary Grace”/“Turpin” gesture most of all — I have marked it text-supported and set out the evidence, rather than assert an intention I cannot verify. A few entries, the razed/raised pair and the recurring “permanent,” are author-confirmed motifs and are labeled as such.

Each entry’s quotation is drawn from the manuscript, and its tier is stated in the entry itself. The point of the concordance is the one stated in The Case: that Mulhern’s allusiveness is demonstrable — countable, categorizable, recurrent — to a degree that places him among the most allusive writers now publishing, and that any account of his craft that ignored it would be incomplete.

The Corpus: A Map

Mulhern’s allusiveness runs across his fiction and poetry alike, and the works interlock — the same story often reappears under a different title in more than one collection, so the concordance reads the works, not the volumes, as the unit of study. The map that follows is the spine of this document.

Novels

  • Molly Bonamici — the domestic-gothic novel of the title character (the corpus’s most heavily allusive single text).
  • Give Them Unquiet Dreams — the pooka novel of Aiden and Martin.

Story cycles

  • The Weight of Small Mercies — five linked Aiden stories: “Arise and Go Now,” “Assumptions,” “Blindfolded,” “Keep Calm and Carry On,” “The Wrong Way.”
  • Useless Things and Other Stories — among them “Useless Things,” “Peppermint Candy,” “A Bath,” and “For Laura” (the chalice story that also appears, retitled, as “Assumptions” in the Aiden cycle).
  • Assumptions and Other Stories — the seven stories treated above (Parts One through Seven).

Poetry

  • Crossings — the lyric collection built around family, faith, Irish-American memory, aging, grief, and thresholds.

Overlaps: “Assumptions,” “Useless Things,” “Myra Bocca,” “Drenched to the Bone,” “Mia Bambina,” and “For Laura”/“Assumptions” occur in more than one volume in differing versions. Where a story has been treated once, the concordance does not repeat the entry; it cross-references.

Five linked Aiden stories. Quotations transcribed from the manuscript. The collection’s own introduction names its debts before a single entry does: “stealing a sacred chalice in hopes of curing his mother, to discovering Yeats and dreams of Ireland,” with “echoes of Frank McCourt, Alice McDermott, and James Joyce’s Dubliners.”

59“Arise and Go Now”

STRONG (title; verbatim echo)literary
the title itself, first words of Yeats’s “The Lake Isle of Innisfree”: *“I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree.”

Literal reading a title for a story about a boy crossing town to visit his grandmother and his institutionalized mother.

Allusive reading Yeats’s poem of arising and going to a healing lake-isle is the story’s buried wish: a child who cannot rise and cannot go, except to the women who hold what is left of home.

Why not decorative the title is not quoted inside the story; it simply is the poem’s first clause, so the reader who knows Yeats hears the whole wish behind the plot, and the reader who does not loses nothing.

60The Cardinal

text-supportedmythic · folk
“That was your grandfather’s favorite bird.” … *“The cardinal flew to a crossbeam above us and looked down, as if it were listening to our conversation.”

Literal reading a red bird that keeps appearing as the boy and his grandmother speak of the dead.

Allusive reading the cardinal is the grandfather’s sign, the dead man returned as bird; the folk belief that cardinals are visiting spirits is relied upon without being stated.

Why not decorative the story itself argues about whether the bird is the ghost (“Have you seen a ghost, Aiden?”); the allusion is held in the same question the characters ask, so the reader is not told which register is true.

61Audrey Rose

STRONG (explicit)literary · cultural
*“She held a book entitled Audrey Rose. A girl in a red dress stood in front of a grave. The ground was on fire.”

Literal reading a paperback held by the grandmother as the boy arrives.

Allusive reading Audrey Rose is the twentieth-century novel and film of reincarnation, a child haunted by another’s death; in the grandmother’s hands it names, by title alone, the story’s central question — whether the dead return.

Why not decorative the book is genuinely there, cover description included, and its subject is the story’s subject; the allusion does the work of epigraph without being lifted to the head of the page.

62“For Laura”

text-supportedonomastic · sacramental
*“As she held the chalice, she murmured, ‘for Laura’ and kissed the side.”

Literal reading a grandmother speaking a name over a stolen cup.

Allusive reading a name said as if in prayer over a sacred vessel, the naming become a kind of lay sacrament; the story elsewhere calls the woman Lorraine, so “Laura” is itself a double of a double.

Why not decorative the murmur is the story’s softest moment and its hinge; the name is offered like the body of the Mass, and the allusion is the gesture, not the word.

63The Chalice

STRONG (explicit)biblical · Arthurian
*“I need to get that chalice, Aiden. I read an article in The Boston Globe that said some people believe it has incredible curing powers. It’s a replica of a chalice from long ago, over 100-years old, with lots of pretty stones on it. If I have your mother drink from it, maybe she’ll get better and come home to us.”

Literal reading a grandmother planning to steal a chalice from her parish altar to cure her daughter.

Allusive reading the chalice is the Grail, the cup of healing, and the story is the Grail quest transposed to a working-class Boston church and a schizophrenic mother.

Why not decorative the cup is physically stolen, and the faith in it is the characters’ own; the legend sits under the plot like the chalice under the grandmother’s coat.

64“The water that gushed from the rock”

STRONG (explicit)biblical
*“You certainly have the power of the Holy Spirit in you. It came out of you like the water that gushed from the rock at Rephidim and Kadesh.”

Literal reading flattery spoken to the priest while the theft is concealed.

Allusive reading Moses striking the rock in the desert to bring forth water, named aloud at the exact moment the grandmother is committing her own sacrilege — the miracle of water invoked over the theft of the cup.

Why not decorative the scripture is the cover for the crime, and the reader who knows the passage sees the irony; the reader who does not still sees a woman lying to a priest.

65“Why aren’t there miracles today?”

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
*“It’s hard to believe that Jesus could have done all that. Why aren’t there miracles today?”

Literal reading a neighbor’s honest doubt at the kitchen sink.

Allusive reading the question is the entire story: a present without miracles, a family reaching for the one miracle it remembers, a chalice stolen to force the miracle that faith no longer supplies.

Why not decorative the line is spoken by a minor character in passing, and it is the thesis the whole collection answers.

66The Dust in the Sunbeams

text-supportedbiblical
“I saw bits of dust in the sunlight streaming through the bay window.” … *“until the sunbeams dimmed and the dust disappeared and her tears stopped.”

Literal reading motes of dust in a shaft of light.

Allusive reading dust again, the collection’s Ash Wednesday token of mortality, made visible and then vanishing in the light.

Why not decorative the image closes the scene with no announcement; the dust is just dust, and that is the point.

67“Useless Things”

STRONG (title)wordplay · motif
*“When you get old you accumulate a lot of useless things, Molly. And eventually you become one of them. So live while you can.”

Allusive reading Poe’s tale of confinement and prey is read in a classroom whose real predator is the teacher herself; the text of torture stands in for the story’s hidden subject.

Part Ten — Molly Bonamici

The novel. Quotations transcribed from the manuscript. Molly’s own adolescent voice states the method before any critic can: “I was always looking for signs.” The entries below are the signs the novel plants and lets the reader keep or miss.

70The Green Light

STRONG (explicit)literary
“we were reading The Great Gatsby in school, a book I despised. Daisy was a pain in the ass and Gatsby was anything but great.” … *“But unlike the dog’s lure, my lure, my green light, would be real—to be as authentic as possible, regardless of what people thought.”

Literal reading Molly’s school reading and her stated ambition.

Allusive reading Gatsby’s green light, the unattainable thing reached for across the water, is claimed and renamed by a girl who has just told us she hates the novel; she rejects the book and keeps its central image as her own.

Why not decorative the allusion works because she quarrels with it; the green light is not borrowed as ornament but argued with, so the reader sees the debt and the defiance in the same sentence.

71Mary in Jesus Christ, Superstar

STRONG (explicit)musical · biblical
*“I was Angela in West Side Story and Mary in Jesus Christ, Superstar. My favorite song in the latter play was ‘I Don’t Know How to Love Him.’ … ‘He’s a man, he’s just a man’ perfectly expressed my feelings about Jesus of Nazareth.”

Literal reading the school plays Molly was cast in.

Allusive reading the novel’s title character is cast, literally, as the Virgin Mary in a rock opera; the woman who cannot love is given the role of the woman who loves God to perfection.

Why not decorative the casting is fact, not symbol, but the irony is structural: the Marian figure of the whole corpus is introduced on a school stage, singing that she does not know how to love.

72Gabriel Callaghan

STRONG (explicit)biblical · onomastic
*“My instructor, Gabriel Callaghan, was a tall dark-skinned Irish guy, close to my age, with thick black hair and blue-green eyes.”

Literal reading the name of Molly’s love interest.

Allusive reading Gabriel is the archangel of the Annunciation, the messenger who announces to Mary; the novel names its Marian protagonist’s beloved “Gabriel” without a word of explanation.

Why not decorative the name does its theological work invisibly; the reader who never hears the angel still has a plausible Irish-American name, and the reader who does hears the Annunciation ringing through a health club.

73“The Dead” at Christmas

STRONG (structural; the names align)literary
“My Michael died five years ago. I’m still trying to get over it.” … Mary Jane plays “Moon River” and then “began singing ‘The Christmas Song’ by Nat King Cole.” … the next morning, at St. Joseph Cemetery, in the snow.

Literal reading a Christmas dinner and a graveside visit with a widowed mother.

Allusive reading Joyce’s “The Dead”: the Christmas party, the dead love named Michael (Joyce’s Michael Furey), the protagonist Gabriel (Joyce’s Gabriel Conroy, and here Gabriel Callaghan), and the snow falling over the dead. The novel’s names and its snow-triangled Christmas are “The Dead” re-enacted without ever naming Joyce.

Why not decorative this is the corpus’s deepest unannounced allusion: two names, a season, and a grave aligned with a story that is never once mentioned, so the echo exists entirely for the reader who already carries “The Dead”.

74Her Own Assumption

STRONG (explicit)biblical · Catholic devotional
“the day that Catholics celebrate the Blessed Mother’s Assumption into heaven.” … “I imagined she was going to demonstrate the Assumption and fly into the sky.” … *“She had created her own Assumption and I thought, ironically, consumption.”

Literal reading the Feast of the Assumption, and an old woman’s death timed to it.

Allusive reading the doctrine of Mary’s bodily assumption is performed by Mrs. Muldoon, whose death on the feast day is her “own Assumption” — and the rhyme “Assumption / consumption” folds the doctrine into the body’s decay.

Why not decorative the doubled word is the novel’s exact method: the sacred ascent and the physical consumption held in one near-rhyme, so the reader hears both meanings at once.

75The Assumption of Mary

STRONG (explicit)art · biblical
*“The Assumption of Mary caught my eye: arms folded against her chest, blue shawl over her white robe, sunlight behind her, surrounded by a sphere of purple clouds.”

Literal reading a stained-glass window in a church.

Allusive reading the window is the novel’s own Titian: the image of the Assumption set in glass, the template the surrounding deaths will re-enact in salt water and in a hot room.

Why not decorative the window is genuinely there, and it is the novel telling us its own icon, the way “Assumptions” tells us Titian’s Assunta, so the image and the plot are one.

76Mary Grace and Turpin

STRONG (structural; *Wise Blood* named)literary
“Mary Grace snickered and opened Flannery O’Connor’s Wise Blood.” … “Mary Grace put down O’Connor’s novel, her face bright red. She took off her glasses; her eyes twitched, and her forehead was sweating. ‘You’re all a bunch of assholes, especially you Turd!’”

Literal reading a disturbed student reading O’Connor and turning on a bully named Leo Turpin.

Allusive reading O’Connor’s “Revelation,” where the fat girl named Mary Grace hurls a book at Mrs. Turpin and calls her a wart hog from hell: the novel re-enacts the gesture with the names and the flung book, while naming only Wise Blood.

Why not decorative the allusion is half-hidden — the novel names one O’Connor book and enacts another — so it rewards exactly the reader who knows O’Connor by name and by gesture.

77“More things in heaven and earth”

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“Or to quote Shakespeare—I loved rereading Hamlet by the way—‘There are more things in heaven and earth, Horatio, than are dreamt of in your philosophy.’”

Literal reading a character quoting Hamlet to argue for openness to the unseen.

Allusive reading Hamlet’s line to Horatio about the limits of philosophy is the novel’s apology for its own ghost-work — the cardinals, the psychics, the returned dead.

Why not decorative the quotation names the novel’s central argument against Molly’s skepticism, and it is allowed to stand as the counterweight to her clever atheism.

78The Fake Last Supper

STRONG (explicit)art · biblical
*“grotesques such as a domed-shaped glass Madonna, figurines of Italian peasants, and the awful fake painting of the Last Supper that hung on the dining room wall.”

Literal reading the kitsch of the family dining room.

Allusive reading the Last Supper, Leonardo’s image of Christ’s final meal, degraded to a “fake” hanging in a room kept shut behind velvet ropes.

Why not decorative the degraded sacred object is the novel’s whole world: the holy held cheaply, behind a cord, still understood to be special enough to rope off.

Part Eleven — Give Them Unquiet Dreams

The pooka novel. Quotations transcribed from the manuscript. Here the allusion is not local but total: the pooka is the novel’s own governing principle, so the single folk-belief is the architecture and the entries below are its load-bearing points.

79The Pooka

STRONG (explicit)mythic · folk
*“A pooka is an evil spirit not to be trusted. In Ireland, the rural folk believed that nature was full of spirits who inhabited every place in the natural world. … If a human disturbs these spaces, the spirits get mad and seek revenge. The dwelling of a pooka is the worst place to unsettle. … A shape shifter, the Irish call them.”

Literal reading a character explaining Irish folk-belief to the boy.

Allusive reading the pooka, the shape-shifting spirit of Irish folklore, is not a passing reference but the novel’s engine: a revenge force that can assume any form.

Why not decorative the spirit is explained, then made to move the plot; the whole novel exists to give the folk-belief flesh, which is allusion used as structure rather than as ornament.

80Second Sight

STRONG (explicit)mythic · folk
“Like you, she has the gift, and it is horrible that she is being punished for it.” … *“Second sight has been in your family for years.”

Literal reading a family inheritance of clairvoyance, passed down like a curse.

Allusive reading the an-da-shealladh, the Celtic gift of second sight, feared and half-believed; the novel lets the gift be real and unbearable at once.

Why not decorative the gift is the lens through which the boy sees everything else; it is not a reference but the narrator’s own instrument.

81“Toreau”

STRONG (explicit; misattributed)literary
*“‘Goodness is the only investment that never fails.’ A smart man by the name of Toreau said that. You must return the chalice to the church.”

Literal reading a dead grandfather’s ghost quoting an aphorism and mangling the name.

Allusive reading Thoreau, misnamed “Toreau” by the dead: the half-remembered name is the form allusion takes among the unlettered, the source worn down to near-recognition.

Why not decorative the misfire is the point; the quotation reaches the boy through a ghost who cannot get the name right, so the debt is felt even where the learning is imperfect.

82“Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John”

STRONG (explicit)biblical
*“There are four angels at my head. Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John,”

Literal reading a child’s bedside prayer.

Allusive reading the four evangelists invoked as guardian angels over a sleeping child, the oldest English prayer against the night.

Why not decorative the prayer is spoken by a boy who can see spirits, so the old invocation against harm is not superstition but the child’s nightly defense — the allusion is the character’s armor.

83“The fish. And the water into wine”

STRONG (explicit)biblical
*“Let’s not forget about the fish. And the water into wine,” my grandmother said.

Literal reading a grandmother listing Christ’s miracles.

Allusive reading the loaves and fishes and the wedding at Cana, the two feeding miracles, named as the family’s household memory of the sacred.

Why not decorative the miracles are spoken as ordinary family shorthand, the way a grandmother might list groceries; the sacred is domestic before it is anything else.

84The Rock That Gushed Water

STRONG (explicit)biblical
*“You certainly have the power of the Holy Spirit in you. It came out of you like the water gushing from the rock at Rephidim and Kadesh.”

Literal reading flattery to a priest, precisely as the chalice is stolen.

Allusive reading Moses striking the rock in the desert; the same line the concordance records in “The Weight of Small Mercies” (entry 64), here replayed in the novel that grew out of those stories.

Why not decorative the recurred line shows the corpus’s true signature: the same allusion carried from story to novel, where it does the same concealed work.

85“A Land Where There Is No Water”

STRONG (explicit)biblical
*“Now I live in a land where there is no water. God help me,” she cried.

Literal reading the pooka-woman’s lament, spoken to the boy.

Allusive reading the wilderness, the place of thirst and exile, the condition of the unshriven; a spirit who exists where there is no water, in a novel built on baptismal water.

Why not decorative the line gives the revenge-spirit her one moment of grief, and it is scriptural—the damned defined by the absence of the water the rest of the book is soaked in.

Part Twelve — Crossings (the poems)

The poems. Quotations transcribed from the manuscript. The lyric side of the same material - family, faith, Irish-American memory, aging, grief, thresholds - in which the allusion is no less structural for being spare.

86“Dark City”

STRONG (title)wordplay · onomastic
*“From Athlone on the Shannon River, / dead center of Ireland - / you walked, / somehow reaching Southampton, / where you boarded the Adriatic, / a name that means dark city.”

Literal reading the grandfather’s emigration, ending on the ship’s deck.

Allusive reading the immigrant vessel, the Adriatic, is glossed by the poem’s own title: the crossing given a dark name, the sea-voyage into an unknown called a city of darkness.

Why not decorative the title and the gloss are the same word; the poem does not borrow a dark city, it discovers that the grandfather’s passage already was one.

87“Songs of Spring”

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“sad like the speaker in Keats’ Ode, / longing for ‘songs of spring,’”

Literal reading the speaker’s weighting sadness.

Allusive reading Keats’s “To Autumn” - “Where are the songs of Spring? Ay, where are they?” - the season’s passing named as loss; the poem borrows the exact phrase for its own grief.

Why not decorative Keats is named, and the quoted phrase is his; the debt is open, and the sadness is shared rather than merely borrowed.

88The Rosary and the Well

STRONG (explicit)biblical · Catholic devotional
*“Most mornings, she knelt, / said her rosary, / then stepped across / a broken-planked porch, / hugged a wooden pail, / and trekked to the water.”

Literal reading the grandmother’s morning ritual, prayer then well.

Allusive reading the rosary and the well conjoined, the two sources of the family’s water - prayer and the drawn pail - in a single morning movement; the well is the sacred source made twice over.

Why not decorative the ritual is recorded flatly, without a single sacred word beyond “rosary,” and the silence is the reverence.

89The Our Father

STRONG (explicit)biblical
*“said an Our Father / kissed his father’s forehead.”

Literal reading a son at his father’s coffin.

Allusive reading the Lord’s Prayer and the dead father collapsed into one gesture: the “Our Father” spoken over the father, the prayer and the man sharing a name in the same line.

Why not decorative the poem’s doubleness is its whole craft here: the sacred word and the mortal man meet in one phrase, and neither displaces the other.

Addendum — the four stories completed

The remaining story entries, transcribed after the main pass: the three further Aiden stories and “A Bath.” They belong to Parts Eight and Nine and are gathered here rather than renumbered across the whole.

90“Hope”

STRONG (explicit)art
“A blindfolded woman wearing a green gown sat atop a light brown globe, her head bent to the left as she played a lyre with a single string.” … *“Printed underneath the reproduction was ‘Hope, 1886, George Frederic Watts.’”

Literal reading a reproduction in the mother’s sketchbook.

Allusive reading Watts’s “Hope”: the blindfolded figure who cannot see, who plays the one string left — the story’s title and the collection’s whole posture, hope held in the dark.

Why not decorative the painting is the last image of the story and the title of it; the blindfold is the condition of the family, and the single string is what remains to be played.

91Elaine and Galahad

STRONG (explicit)literary · Arthurian
*“‘I’m Elaine, and this is my grandson Galahad.’”

Literal reading false names given as the stolen chalice is returned.

Allusive reading Elaine of Astolat and Galahad, the purest knight of the Grail quest; the pair who return the Grail give each other Grail names, so the Arthurian legend is stated in the one moment it is acted out.

Why not decorative the names are a lie (the grandmother is not Elaine), yet truer than the truth: the chalice is the Grail, and the boy is its innocent knight.

92“Children of God”

STRONG (explicit; 1 John 3:1)biblical
*“‘See what love the Father has bestowed on us that we may be called the children of God. Yet so we are.’”

Literal reading the priest reading scripture as the chalice is returned.

Allusive reading the epistle on adopted divine sonship is read at the exact moment the family reclaims its daughter; the “children of God” are the boy and his mother being reunited.

Why not decorative the lectionary is not chosen for the scene; it simply is the scene, and the story lets the scripture say what the plot is already doing.

93Keep Calm and Carry On

STRONG (explicit)historical · colloquial
“You know what the British people used to say when the Germans bombed London during World War II?” … *“Keep calm and carry on.”

Literal reading an old slogan the grandmother teaches the boy.

Allusive reading the Ministry of Information’s stoic poster, spoken as the family’s own creed of endurance under bombardment.

Why not decorative the slogan is explained, then made the title, then whispered at the story’s close to “me or to herself. Or to both of us” — philosophy and comfort in one borrowed phrase.

94“There but for the grace of God go I”

STRONG (explicit)biblical · colloquial
*“Say it with me. ‘There but for the grace of God go I.’”

Literal reading a grandmother teaching her grandson a prayer as they give charity.

Allusive reading the old acknowledgment — it is grace, not merit, that keeps one from the neighbor’s misfortune — made into a shared recitation.

Why not decorative the sentence is the story’s theology of the poor; it is said, together, so the doctrine is an act rather than an ornament.

95Lorraine / Laura

STRONG (explicit)onomastic
*“That was my mother’s name, her formal name. I’ve told you a thousand times to call her Laura.”

Literal reading a quarrel over what to call the hospitalized daughter.

Allusive reading the double name of the whole cycle resolved: Lorraine is the formal name, Laura the spoken one, so the murmur “for Laura” (entry 62) is the prayer said over the woman’s true name, not a slip.

Why not decorative the name is argued over on the page, which is the story admitting that naming itself is the sacrament it has been performing all along.

96Dickens’s Ghosts

STRONG (explicit)literary
“Scrooge sees ghosts.” … *“I think you’re a sad guy, like that Ghost of Christmas Past.”

Literal reading a boy and his father discussing Great Expectations and A Christmas Carol.

Allusive reading Dickens runs the length of the story: Pip and the convict in the graveyard mirror the boy’s own abandonment, and the father who leaves for Arizona is named the Ghost of Christmas Past — Dickens’s ghosts set against the mother’s “ghosts” the father will not believe in.

Why not decorative the allusion is the argument itself; the boy uses Dickens to defend his mother’s visions, and the fiction is shown to be one way the true is told.

97“The Wrong Way”

STRONG (title)wordplay · structural
*“You’re going the wrong way.”

Literal reading a boy correcting his father’s driving.

Allusive reading the title is the father’s literal mistake and his moral one — the road to Arizona, away from the family — so geography and conscience collapse into one phrase.

Why not decorative the direction and the abandonment are the same word; the story never needs to say which “wrong way” it means.

98“All We Ever Needed”

STRONG (title; the thesis line)motif · structural
*“For a few moments, the three of us had all we ever needed.”

Literal reading a mother, son, and grandmother together in a kitchen.

Allusive reading the sentence is the collection’s title and its whole argument: what the family needs is not the chalice or the cure, but this — the ordinary held for a moment.

Why not decorative the closing line states the thesis in the plainest possible words; the allusion is to the work’s own title, so the text and its meaning are one.

99The Bath

STRONG (explicit)biblical · Catholic devotional
“The only swimming I do now is in the bathtub.” … *“That was Clew Bay in County Mayo.”

Literal reading a grandmother helped into her bath.

Allusive reading the great water of the grandmother’s youth — Clew Bay, where she once waded for sea lettuce — reduced to a tub; the daughter now bathes the mother, and the ocean becomes the ablution.

Why not decorative the bath is literal and the baptism silent; the water of the entire corpus is poured into one enamel tub, and the story lets it stand.

100Glencar

text-supportedliterary · onomastic
*“We’re here to visit my daughter, Laura Glencar.”

Literal reading the mother’s surname.

Allusive reading Glencar is Yeats’s landscape in “The Stolen Child,” where “the wandering water gushes / From the hills above Glen-Car”; the name of the woman lost to visions carries the poem of the child stolen by the fairies.

Why not decorative the name is not pointed out, only given, so the echo waits for the reader who knows the poem — the strictest form the corpus’s method takes.

Addendum II — Further Examples

Entries 101 onward, transcribed in the deeper pass: the named sources the first pass had not yet quoted.

101Joyce by Name

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“...the James Joyce House of the Dead across from the River Liffey, and Trinity College, where we saw the Book of Kells.”

Literal reading a Dublin sightseeing list.

Allusive reading the novel that re-enacts “The Dead” through Gabriel and Michael (entry 73) at last names Joyce outright, at the very house where “The Dead” is set. “The dead” are named twice — the street of the living and the title of the story — as if the city itself carried the allusion.

Why not decorative the name appears only once, in a travel list, so the reader who has felt the echo all along meets its source at the last possible moment, the way an allusion should arrive.

102Innisfree, Quoted in Full

STRONG (explicit; the poem transcribed)literary
*“Someone, I suppose my grandmother, had copied a poem by William Butler Yeats—‘The Lake Isle of Innisfree.’ / I will arise and go now, and go to Innisfree, / And a small cabin build there, of clay and wattles made; / Nine bean-rows will I have there, a hive for the honey-bee, / And live alone in the bee-loud glade.”

Literal reading a handwritten copy of the poem found among the grandmother’s things.

Allusive reading the title “Arise and Go Now” (entry 59) is shown to be Yeats in full: the grandmother wrote the poem out by hand, so the allusion the reader has been hearing all along is, in the end, a real page.

Why not decorative the poem is not quoted about the family; it is copied by the family, so the allusion is an heirloom rather than an ornament.

103Dickinson’s Epigraph

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“The distance that the dead have gone does not at first appear— / Their coming back seems possible for many an ardent year. —Emily Dickinson”

Literal reading an epigraph at the head of a chapter.

Allusive reading Dickinson’s line on the nearness of the dead sets the chapter’s subject — the ghosts, the second sight — before a word of prose; the “coming back” the poem wonders about is the book’s very plot.

Why not decorative the epigraph does the work of a threshold, saying in advance what the chapter will enact, and Dickinson appears here alongside her other appearance in the corpus (“Tell all the truth but tell it slant,” entry 42).

104Rossetti’s Damsel of the Grail

STRONG (explicit)art · Arthurian
*“Underneath was a gold plaque: ‘The Damsel of the Holy Grail—Dante Gabriel Rossetti, 1874.’”

Literal reading a painting’s plaque in a museum.

Allusive reading Rossetti’s Pre-Raphaelite damsel of the Grail stands beside Watts’s “Hope” (entry 90): the corpus pictures the Grail through actual Pre-Raphaelite canvases, so the Arthurian material is always seen through Victorian art.

Why not decorative the painting is present in the scene, unremarked beyond its plaque, and the reader who knows the Rossetti sees the Grail legend the way the novel keeps showing it — framed and hanging on a wall.

105The Scarlet Letter

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“‘Shakespeare?’ / She smiled. ‘Good guess. But it was Nathaniel Hawthorne in The Scarlet Letter. You’ll read the book in high school.’”

Literal reading a grandmother testing and correcting a guest’s guess.

Allusive reading the guess of Shakespeare corrected to Hawthorne: the novel lets a character be wrong about an allusion and sets it right, a lesson in the naming of sources the whole corpus practices.

Why not decorative the error and its correction teach the reader how this world alludes — by near-miss and recovery — rather than by smooth quotation.

106Time Is a Stream

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“Like Thoreau, I believe time is merely a stream we swim in. Someday the current of water will slip away, taking us with it, but the sandy bottom, eternity, will remain.”

Literal reading a meditation on time held beside a river.

Allusive reading a second Thoreau, distinct from the grandfather’s “Goodness is the only investment” (entry 81): here Thoreau’s river becomes the novel’s own image of time, hope, and love.

Why not decorative the allusion is the entire passage’s frame; the thought is built inside Thoreau’s metaphor, so the debt and the idea are one.

107The Boston Latin Pantheon

STRONG (explicit)literary · historical
*“Some of the distinguished alumni are Cotton Mather, Benjamin Franklin, Samuel Adams, John Hancock, Charles Bullfinch, and Ralph Waldo Emerson.”

Literal reading a teacher listing his school’s famous graduates.

Allusive reading the American literary and political pantheon — Mather, Franklin, Adams, Emerson — invoked as the boy’s own school’s inheritance, so the national tradition is made the personal one.

Why not decorative the list is a fact of the school, and it plants the novel’s heroes in the narrator’s own hall; Emerson appears here as the same figure the Molly stories quote elsewhere.

108The Catalogue of Totems

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“I’d like to be / the Wife of Bath’s scarlet stockings / poor Yorick’s skull / Dickinson’s sherry in the glass / Gatsby’s dock / Hulga’s wooden leg”

Literal reading a poem listing objects the speaker wishes to be.

Allusive reading five allusions in five lines: Chaucer’s Wife of Bath (her scarlet hose), Shakespeare’s Yorick (Hamlet), Dickinson’s sherry, Gatsby’s dock (the green light), and O’Connor’s Hulga (the wooden leg of “Good Country People”). The poem is a totem-pole of the corpus’s reach.

Why not decorative the whole poem is allusion, each object a line from a different master; the speaker asks to become the very echoes this concordance has been tracing, one per line.

109The Dickinson Dash

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“I long to be singular— distinct as a Dickinson dash.”

Literal reading a wish to be unlike others.

Allusive reading Dickinson’s dash — the poet’s signature mark, the pause that sets her apart — made the figure of individuality itself.

Why not decorative the image is drawn from the very typography of the borrowed poet; the mark and the meaning are one.

110Thoreau’s Wood Thrush

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“In a forest, sitting on a sycamore, Thoreau’s wood thrush sings.”

Literal reading a bird in the woods.

Allusive reading the wood thrush is Thoreau’s bird, the singer of Walden evening; naming it “Thoreau’s” makes the natural world his text.

Why not decorative the bird is real and the debt is explicit in a single possessive, the calmest form an allusion can take.

111Coleridge and Keats

STRONG (explicit)literary
*“Lines of Coleridge or Keats— words you love.”

Literal reading a poet’s remembered reading.

Allusive reading the Romantics held as the words one loves, the canon as comfort rather than burden.

Why not decorative the names are offered without a quotation, so the allusion is the company the poem keeps, not a borrowing it must justify.

Addendum III — Allusive Names

The names the corpus gives its people. Names are the densest vein of all: they carry the allusion silently, and most of them are never glossed.

112Molly — the diminutive of Mary

STRONG (etymology; the corpus enacts it)onomastic · Marian
“My name is Molly Bonamici.” … and the novel: *“I was Angela in West Side Story and Mary in Jesus Christ, Superstar.”

Literal reading a girl’s name, and a school-play cast list.

Allusive reading Molly is the old English diminutive of Mary: the corpus’s central figure is named for the Virgin, hidden in a nickname. The novel makes the secret visible once, casting her literally as Mary on a stage; everywhere else the Marian name runs as a buried current under the Marian plot.

Why not decorative the name is not a flourish but the character’s whole design: a woman called Mary, who does not believe, who is made to play Mary, in a body of work built on the Assumption.

113Bonamici — “good friends”

STRONG (explicit; glossed twice)onomastic
“our restaurant eponymously named ‘Bonamici’s,’ which means, by the way, ‘good friends.’ I have always found our surname ironic because my father was an unhappy person who rarely smiled.” … *“‘Your last name means ”good friends.“‘”

Literal reading a surname, and its meaning stated in the text.

Allusive reading a sociopath whose name means “good friends”: the woman who cannot feel is named for the bond she counterfeits, and the text itself calls the surname “ironic.”

Why not decorative the name is glossed by the narrator, so the irony is the character’s own discovery, not the critic’s; the allusion is the self of the protagonist.

114Agnella — the lamb

text-supportedonomastic · biblical
*“Call me Agnella. Janssen is my married name.”

Literal reading the grandmother correcting the doctor to her first name.

Allusive reading agnella is the Italian for a female lamb; the grandmother is named for the lamb — the Agnus Dei, the Lamb of God — in a corpus where “fleecy” and “cradle” (entry 6) keep the Nativity’s lamb-language running.

Why not decorative the name is never glossed; it sits in the text as a silent given, the way the sacred does everywhere else in this work.

115Joshua

STRONG (explicit)onomastic · biblical
“You could call Joshua that. He works for a reputable institution.” … *“Jerusalem, New York.”

Literal reading a grandmother inventing a museum curator to explain the stolen chalice.

Allusive reading Joshua is the Hebrew name of Jesus (Yeshua), paired here with Jerusalem and the healing cup; the grandmother conjures a man named Christ, from the holy city, to vouch for the Grail — the name that is God, used as a lie.

Why not decorative the allusion is blasphemy doing the work of plot: the name of Christ is the cover story for a theft, and the irony is the scene’s entire weight.

116Finnegan

text-supportedonomastic · literary
*“Emily Finnegan. And this is my mother.”

Literal reading a roommate’s introduction.

Allusive reading Finnegan is the surname at the center of Joyce’s Finnegans Wake; a living Finnegan moves through a corpus that re-enacts “The Dead” (entries 73, 101), so the name is one more Joycean hinge the text never announces.

Why not decorative the name costs nothing to carry and everything to catch; it is the allusion as a surname, worn like an ordinary coat.

117Grace

STRONG (explicit)onomastic · theological
“That’s not true. I visit you at least once every two weeks.” … *“I suppose you do, Grace.”

Literal reading the mother’s name, in an argument about visitations.

Allusive reading the battered mother in “A Bath” is named Grace; the word runs the whole corpus’s ethic of mercy (“There but for the grace of God go I,” entry 94), so the woman who survives is named for the grace that spares her.

Why not decorative the name is spoken only in rebuke and tenderness, never explained, and the story lets “Grace” mean what the theology has meant all along.

118Mary Grace

STRONG (explicit)onomastic · biblical
*“Mary Grace snickers and opens Flannery O’Connor’s Wise Blood.”

Literal reading a student’s name.

Allusive reading Mary plus Grace: the Virgin and the Hail Mary’s “full of grace” folded into a single name, worn by the girl who quotes Romans and Matthew (entries 54–55) and who re-enacts O’Connor’s “Revelation.”

Why not decorative the double-sacred name is O’Connor’s own choice, and the corpus borrows it whole, so the character arrives already carrying her allusion.

119Peggy — the pearl

text-supportedonomastic · biblical
“I always thought it a cruel irony that she had the same name as the graceful and beautiful skater.” … and, in the Aiden cycle: *“Sean and I were the only people who knew about the pearls.”

Literal reading a nickname, and a dead man’s secret.

Allusive reading Peggy is the diminutive of Margaret, whose name means “pearl”; the pearls the grandfather hid (entry on the pearls) are the literal form of the name, so “Peggy” and the pearls are the same word in two keys — one a woman, one a buried treasure.

Why not decorative the connection is never spoken; the name and the object sit in the two collections and rhyme silently, the way the pearl of great price hides in the field.

This completes the corpus. The concordance now reads the stories, the Aiden cycle, both novels, and the poems as one continuous habit - a writer whose allusiveness is not a mannerism but a method, held together by a single conviction: that the allusion is literally true first, and that the echo only ever adds a register to a surface that already stands. The place to read that claim tested is the whole document, line by line, and it invites the check.

A final note on method, unchanged: the concordance claims only what it can demonstrate from the text. Where a source is named or quoted, the tier is STRONG; where the echo is structural but unannounced, it is marked and the evidence set out; where a manuscript has not yet been transcribed, the entry says so rather than inventing a quotation. That is the honest shape of the whole corpus, and it is the only shape worth publishing.
