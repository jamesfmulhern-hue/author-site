The Breath at the End: Reading “The Wrong Way”

On the felt heart, dissolving breath, biblical paths, Whitman, and Emersonian air.

By James F. Mulhern

The Wrong Way ends with an action completed but not explained away:

We leaned the shovels against the side of the house and walked up the steps. I felt the beating of my heart and the passing of air into my lungs. The smoke of my breath rose and dissolved like eddies in a careless sky.

The shovels have been set aside, and the narrative approaches the house. Yet the scene does not close upon domestic safety or moral clarification. It pauses with the body: pulse, inhalation, exhalation. Then it passes outward, through visible breath, into a sky whose carelessness neither consoles nor condemns.

The Felt Heart

“I felt the beating of my heart” is deliberately plain, but it is not merely physiological. The speaker does not simply say that his heart beat; he says that he felt the beating. An unconscious rhythm becomes an object of attention. After work and uncertainty, the self becomes a witness to its own continuance: still alive, still vulnerable, still situated in time.

That quiet allusive pressure matters. The sentence draws strength from the literary and devotional tradition in which a perceived heartbeat becomes an elementary proof of creaturely existence. It does not announce a revelation. It registers the smallest possible one: the body persists, even when an account of what has happened remains incomplete.

Breath and Eddies

The final image transfers this inward awareness to the cold air. Breath becomes visible as smoke; it rises, turns, and disappears. The gesture is Whitmanesque not because it mechanically imitates Whitman's manner, but because the individual body is allowed to meet the material universe without a hard boundary between them. What comes from within the speaker enters the shared air, takes a form for a moment, and is absorbed.

“Eddies” is the sentence's decisive word. An eddy is a temporary turning inside a larger current, a shape made by forces it does not command. It therefore quietly revises the title. A “wrong way” suggests a mistaken line of travel; the eddies refuse such simple linearity. They are neither a straight road nor a stable destination. They are momentary patterns of movement, contingent on wind, weather, and an open world.

The Biblical Way

The title also bears biblical pressure. In Scripture, a way is often a moral and spiritual course, not merely a road: the narrow way and the broad way; the crooked made straight; the wanderer called to turn back. “The Wrong Way” can therefore name more than directional error. It can suggest a life bent out of true alignment by choice, burden, circumstance, grief, or all four at once.

Yet the biblical resonance complicates condemnation. Scriptural language imagines restoration as well as error: the crooked may be straightened, the bowed lifted, and the broken met with nearness and consolation. The title can contain moral seriousness without making the story a verdict. Against the traditional image of a path made straight, the final eddies become especially moving. The breath does not travel along a clear line of correction. It turns briefly within currents beyond the speaker's control and then dissolves.

A Careless Sky

The “careless sky” carries an Emersonian doubleness, recalling the “blithe air” of Nature. Careless can mean indifferent to human fear, labor, memory, and blame. But it can also mean free of care: unburdened, unpossessive, released from the human need to make every event yield a moral accounting.

That doubleness prevents the final image from becoming simply bleak. The sky does not ratify the speaker's anxiety, but it does not erase him. It receives his breath for an instant. The result is an ending of witness rather than solution: the tools are put away, the speaker moves toward the house, the heart continues, and breath enters the world before it is gone.

Allusion as Resonance

These connections—to biblical images of the way, to Whitman's porous body and cosmos, and to Emerson's untroubled air—are best understood as resonances rather than as a set of declared quotations. The passage first works through its sensory precision: pulse, lungs, cold vapor, turning eddies, sky. Its larger literary and spiritual field remains implicit, available to the reader after the fact.

The ending does not insist that there is, finally, a single right way. It offers something more modest and durable: a consciousness briefly attentive to life itself, and a breath that rises beyond possession into a world that does not arrange itself around human need.
