A Recurring Series

From the Work

Close readings of the craft and the belief in the published work. Each installment gathers one strong passage, follows what it is doing line by line, and names the philosophy doing the work underneath — recurrence, mercy, the sacred carried by an ordinary gesture. The voice is the voice of a careful reader; the authority is the prose itself, not a signature.

September 2026 From A Prayer for Home

What a dropped page can carry

“What happened to Mrs. Muldoon’s husband?” I asked.

Nonna said, “Before you were born, Mr. Muldoon died from a massive heart attack. Poor Mary was fixing dinner in the kitchen. When she called him to the table, he didn’t answer. She went into the living room, where he would listen to the radio and read the paper, and found him slumped over, the paper scattered at his feet. She hasn’t been the same since. Just drinks away her sorrows.”

“Oh,” I answered. I couldn’t comprehend what it would be like to find someone dead, especially a husband or a family member. — A Prayer for Home

The passage earns its grief through a child’s question and an adult’s flat answer, and it keeps the death inside a single ordinary object. The man is not described dying; he is described by what lies at his feet — the newspaper he was reading when the heart stopped. That scattered paper does the work an elegy might do, and does it with the plainness the scene requires: death arrives not as event but as aftermath, not as blood but as a dropped page. It is the characteristic move, the terrible carried by an ordinary noun and left there.

The craft is in the dialogue’s economy. The exchange carries no explanation, no editorial comment, only a child’s two-letter answer — “Oh” — followed by an admission the adult narrator recalls precisely: “I couldn’t comprehend what it would be like to find someone dead, especially a husband or a family member.” That sentence is what the remembering voice allows: the child’s sensory filter held in place, the adult’s cadence shaping it years later. The word especially does the quiet work. The child has already begun to rank the unrankable, and the ranking is the first lesson in mortality.

Beneath the plain speech runs the story’s deeper subject. The grown-ups do what the people in this body of work always do — pass grief sideways, in gossip, in the flat recitation of another woman’s ruin, while the child listens at the table’s edge. “She hasn’t been the same since. Just drinks away her sorrows.” Nothing is explained; everything is carried forward. Grief is not argued toward, only recounted — and the recounting, like the scattered paper, is what remains.
